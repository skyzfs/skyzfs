# M1.3j: the read path, options 3 and 4 compared

For the lead to decide from. No code. Written from reading the store at 530bd7c (`crates/skyzfs-local/src/store.rs`, `log.rs`, the pump) and from the M1.T10 numbers the lead sent (four runs on slow runners, one run per figure). Where I am unsure it says "unsure".

**Option 3**: the checkpoint step runs on a thread of its own. **Option 4**: reads do not go through the store's worker.

## The short of it

- The numbers say a read's 259 to 280 µs in the daemon are: 127 to 138 µs behind a step or the rule's own sync, 64 µs in the two thread hand-overs, 31 to 38 µs its own call, the rest behind other requests.
- Option 4 with one reader thread removes everything a read waits for on the worker (the step, the own sync, writes, flushes): my estimate is about 110 to 125 µs left of 270. It writes nothing, so what is on the disk at any instant is exactly what it is today, and every kill and power-cut argument stands as it is. It changes who may read the index and needs one new guard (a pin) with two places where the worker waits for readers.
- Option 3 removes the step's walk, save, apply and home sync from the worker's path for every request, writes and flushes included. With the log's new start left on the worker, a read still waits for that and for the own sync (24 + 9.5 µs of today's 127 to 138): my estimate is about 165 to 180 µs left. It changes when things are written relative to each other, which is what the simulated disk's single order and the cut tests rest on; the test model is the larger part of the work.
- If they are staged I would build option 4 with a reader thread first, then decide on option 3 from the writes' and flushes' waits, which I have not seen.
- Neither is needed to try two smaller things the numbers point at; they are at the end, not asked for.

The estimates are sums of the lead's means with the parts removed that an option removes. They assume a part that is removed leaves nothing in its place. They are not measurements.

## What exists today (for both options)

- **Threads.** The pump's thread (the queue, the translation, the commit, the acknowledged position's record) and the store's worker, which owns `Core`: the log, the home image, the preimage file, the index. They share nothing but two channels and the shown state (one mutex, held for a copy).
- **A read** (`Core::read`): the index gives, for the read's blocks, runs that are "in the log at this record", "zeros" or nothing. A log run is two `pread`s on the segment file (the record's header, checked for being a record at that position, then the payload); nothing is a `pread` on the home image; zeros touch no file. All reads are buffered.
- **A step** (`Core::apply`): (0) walk the log to choose the batch; (1) save the neighbours of what it will write, in the preimage file, synced; (2) write the batch's records into the home image, deallocating for write-zeroes; (3) sync the home image; (4) write the log's new start (the superblock, with its sync), which frees the segment files below it; (5) the index lets go of everything below the new start.
- **The log's segment files are reused, not deleted.** A freed slot is taken by the next roll and overwritten. This is the fact that makes a reader outside the worker dangerous, below.
- **The property every option leans on**: the blocks a step writes into the home image are in the index, pointing at the log, until (5). So a read that begins while a step runs gets those blocks from the log, and a read that goes to the home image reads blocks the step does not write.
- **What the contract asks of a read** (R3): a read received after an operation was acknowledged observes it; a read that overlaps an operation in flight sees, *for each block*, the content before it or after it. So a read need not be whole against a write in flight, only block by block. R8's "whole, in one total order" is about mutations.

## 1. Threads, what they share, and the guards

### Option 3

| thing | who touches it | guard, and how long |
|---|---|---|
| index | the worker only, as today | none |
| log: appending, rolling, syncing, the superblock | the worker only (variant 3a) | none |
| log: the records of the batch, read by position | the step's thread | none needed, if the worker hands the step's thread the batch's range and the file handles of its segments when it starts the step: those segments cannot be freed before the start moves, which only the worker does, after the step. The worker appends beyond the batch in the same or a later file; a `pread` below the durable position beside an append beyond it is safe |
| home image | the step's thread writes, deallocates and syncs; the worker reads | none: by the property above a worker's read never reads a block the running step writes, and the worker starts the next step itself, when no read of its own is under way |
| preimage file | the step's thread only | none |
| "the volume failed" | both | an atomic flag; the step's thread reads it before each write, the worker before each request |
| the step's result | step's thread to worker | a channel message "home image durable up to X", or "failed" |

- **Variant 3a (my recommendation within option 3)**: the worker moves the log's start and drops the index when the step's thread reports. The superblock's write and sync stay on the worker: 2.71 ms per step in the numbers.
- **Variant 3b**: the step's thread moves the start too. Then the log is shared: the superblock is written by a roll on the worker and by a reclaim on the step's thread, and the table of live segments is changed by both. It needs a lock around the superblock and that table, held for the superblock's sync (milliseconds) and taken by every roll. I would not start here.
- Unsure: the log's reader keeps state as it walks (a read cache and the chain of tags). The step's thread needs its own instance of that. I have not checked how much of `Log` must be split to give it one.

### Option 4

| thing | who touches it | guard, and how long |
|---|---|---|
| index | the worker inserts (every mutation) and drops (step's end); readers look up | a read-write lock, held by a reader for the lookup only (a tree walk, well under a microsecond) and by the worker for an insert or the drop. Not held across a `pread` |
| log's segment files | the worker appends, rolls, reuses freed slots; readers `pread` a record's payload | **a pin** (below): a slot is not reused while a reader that looked up before the index dropped is still reading |
| home image | the worker's step writes; readers `pread` | **the same pin**: a step does not begin writing while a reader that looked up before the batch was chosen is still reading |
| preimage file, superblock, everything else of the log | the worker only | none |
| "the volume failed" | all | an atomic flag. A reader that cannot read reports to the worker, which marks the log as today, and the read's failure is delivered after that |

**The pin.** One read-write lock used the other way round: a reader holds it shared from just before its lookup to the end of its last `pread`; the worker takes it exclusively for an instant, as a barrier, at two points of a step: before the first write into the home image, and after the index has dropped and before any slot freed by this step may be reused. The barrier waits for the readers under way, which is as long as the slowest read: microseconds from the page cache, a disk read on a miss (the bounded-cache rows have a mean call of 151 to 171 µs). It happens twice per step, about five times a second. New readers that arrive during a barrier wait for it, an instant.

## 2. What a reader needs to hold true

**While a write appends.** The worker appends the record, then inserts into the index, then sends the completion. A read received after the write was acknowledged looks up after the insert, on any thread, because its lookup is after its own submission. That is all R3 asks, and it needs only that an insert is atomic for a lookup (the index's lock).

**A read that overlaps a write submitted before it and not yet appended.** Today the single order makes the read see the write. R3 does not ask for that: the write is in flight, and each block may be before or after it. With the lookup under the index's lock the read sees a state of the index at one instant, so in fact it sees each mutation wholly or not at all, which is more than R3 asks. What replaces the single order is therefore nothing but "look up after you were submitted". Unsure: whether the conformance suite's model and `skyzfs-blkcheck` accept "before" for a read overlapping a write in flight, as R3's text does, or have come to expect "after" because the store always gave it. That must be read before any code; if they expect "after", the contract and the checkers disagree today and the lead decides which gives.

**A reader that has looked up a position in the log and has not read it when the step frees that segment** (option 4 only; in option 3 reads are on the worker, which frees). Without a guard: the step moves the start, the index drops, a later roll reuses the slot and overwrites it; the reader's header check may or may not notice, and between its two `pread`s nothing notices. Today's `Log::read_payload` also refuses a position below the start, which would fail the volume for a reader that was merely slow. So: the reader resolves the record to a file handle and offset under the pin and reads without asking the log's start again, and the pin's second barrier keeps the slot from reuse until that reader is done. Dropping the index before the barrier is what makes the barrier enough: a reader that begins after the drop cannot find the old position.

**A read of a block the step is writing into the home image.**
- Option 3: cannot happen, by the property above and because the worker both reads and starts steps.
- Option 4: a reader that begins after the batch's records were inserted finds the log. A reader that looked up *before* a write was appended can still be reading the home image when a step applies that write there. R3 allows the block's old or new content, but a buffered read racing a buffered write into the same page can return a block that is part old and part new (the kernel does not lock a page that is up to date for a read; I am fairly sure of this and have not tested it). The pin's first barrier closes it: the step waits for the readers that looked up before its batch was chosen. Deallocation counts as a write here.

**While the step syncs the home image, moves the start, drops the index.** The sync changes no content. Between the start moving and the index dropping, a lookup still says "log" for the batch's blocks, and the segments are still there (freed, not yet reused): correct. After the drop a lookup says "home", which is durable and holds the batch. The order "home written, home synced, start moved, index dropped, then slots reusable" must not change; `INDEX_SWITCH_EARLY` and `RECLAIM_BEFORE_HOME_SYNC` are the mutants that hold the first parts of it today.

**A failed volume** (R11): a request in flight when the log fails may complete either way, so a reader that is under way needs no stop. "From the first failure reported, every later request fails, reads included" needs the flag set before the failed completion is delivered, on whichever thread.

## 3. Option 4 in three shapes

| | a page-cache miss | hand-overs per read | the `BlockTarget` contract and the conformance suite |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. the pump's thread reads** (inside `submit`, the completion kept for the next `poll`) | Stops the pump: no request is fetched, submitted or committed until the disk answers. Every other request waits for it, writes' commits included | none | The contract's text holds (a request completes once, through `poll`). The suite runs unchanged against it. But the suite's simulated disk never blocks, so nothing in it shows the stall |
| **B. one or more reader threads** | Stops that reader. With one reader, reads wait behind it as they do today behind the worker ("reads behind reads", 63 to 84 µs in the bounded-cache rows); with several, misses overlap. Nothing else waits | two, as today (to the reader, back to the pump); but the reader is idle unless it reads, so the wait is the wake-up alone | Unchanged. The suite runs unchanged; its threaded harness already drives a served store |
| **C. io_uring reads on the pump's ring into the request's buffer** | The kernel's business: nothing in the daemon waits | none; the completion arrives in the wait the pump already makes | **Changed.** The target no longer executes a read: it would have to say where a read's blocks are (several extents per request: log payloads, home ranges, zeros) and hold the pin until the pump says the reads are done. That is a new operation in the contract (design §4.1 and §5.1), and the conformance suite, which drives `submit` and `poll`, would not be testing the path the daemon uses unless it gets an adapter that performs the planned reads. The ring code belongs in `skyzfs-ublk-sys` (the only crate with `unsafe`), and the simulated disk has no ring |

- Shape A is the smallest and the fastest on a warm cache (it saves both hand-overs, 64 µs) and the worst on a cold one. The bounded-cache rows are the cold case, and M1's criterion includes them, so I would not build A.
- Shape B with one reader is the least change that removes the waits behind the worker. The number of readers is then a parameter the bounded-cache rows can set.
- Shape C is where the two hand-overs go away without a stall. It is the largest by far and the only one that changes the contract. Unsure: whether the pump's buffer for a request is memory the ring can read into directly; I did not check how `take_buf` is backed.
- Expected life of a read, from the lead's means: B with one reader about 9 (to submit) + 30 (wake-up) + 10 to 16 (behind other reads) + 31 to 38 (call) + 34 (ready to taken) + 1, so 115 to 128 µs; A about 9 + 31 to 38 + 1 on a warm cache; C about the same as A plus the kernel's own cost, which I cannot estimate.
- One behaviour changes in every shape: today a read submitted behind a write that is held (the log full behind a hold) is held with it; `a_full_log_holds_writes_behind_a_hold_and_goes_on_without_one` says so. With reads off the worker the read is answered. R8 does not ask for the old behaviour, but the test's text and expectation change, on purpose.

## 4. Option 3: what the worker still does, what a read still waits for

- The worker still does, per step: choosing when to start it and handing over the batch's range (microseconds); the log's new start (2.71 ms, with the superblock's sync); dropping the index (0.02 ms); and the rule's own sync of the log when there is too little durable to take (1.08 ms).
- Unsure whether the walk that chooses the batch (3.05 ms) can move to the step's thread whole. It reads the log's records, which the step's thread can do; but the batch's end must be fixed against the durable position and the hold, which are the worker's. I believe the worker can pass those two positions and let the step's thread walk; I have not tried it.
- A read then still waits for: the worker's wake-up (30 µs), other reads (10 to 16), writes (4 to 8), flushes (6 to 7), other (3 to 4), and of the step: the log's start (24 µs today) and the own sync (9.5). Together about 87 to 99 µs of wait where there are 183 to 199 today; a read's life about 165 to 180 µs.
- The two hand-overs stay.
- Unsure: whether the home image's sync on one thread slows the log's sync on another. Both files are on one file system, and on a journalling file system one file's sync can wait for a commit the other started. Only a kernel run says.

## 5. The write path and a flush

**Option 3.** Writes and flushes stop waiting behind the walk, save, apply and home sync, as reads do. That is its advantage over option 4: a synchronous write is an append and a flush, and today a flush that arrives during a step waits for it. The cost: appends now arrive while a step runs, so the bound on the log changes shape. Today, at the high mark, the worker takes steps before the next request, and the log stays below the high mark plus one request. With the step elsewhere the worker must hold back *appends* (reads and flushes go on) while the log is at the high mark and a step is under way, and take them again when the step reports. The mechanism for holding a request exists (the log full behind a hold). The gate was never engaged in the M1.T10 rows (count 0), so this is about keeping the bound, not about speed. `make_room`, the explicit checkpoint and the hold all move the log's start: they go through the step's thread or wait for it.

**Option 4.** The write path and the flush are untouched and wait behind a step as today. Two small effects: every mutation takes the index's lock for its insert (uncontended, tens of nanoseconds), and a step waits twice for the readers under way. Writes also no longer wait behind reads' calls (4 to 8 µs of a read's wait is behind writes today; I have not seen the writes' wait behind reads).

## 6. What the tests and rigs must then show, and what loses its meaning

**Option 4.**
- What is on the disk, and in what order it gets there, does not change: readers write nothing. The simulated disk's single seeded order of writes, the cut tests, the kill tests and the replay rig's argument are untouched. This is the main reason I put it first.
- New, to be shown: (a) a reader paused between its lookup and its `pread` of a log record, while a step runs to its end and later appends roll into new segments: the freed slot is not reused until the reader is released, and the reader returns the record's data; (b) a reader paused between its lookup and its `pread` of the home image, while a write to the same block is appended and acknowledged and a step is due: the step does not write until the reader is released, and the reader returns a block that is wholly old; (c) a read submitted after a write's acknowledgement sees it, under load, with the reader and the worker really racing; (d) the conformance suite through the served store with readers, as now.
- New hooks: a pause in the reader after the lookup (as the step's pauses today). New mutants: the slot reused without the barrier; the home image written without the barrier; the lookup taken before the read's own submission is not expressible, so no mutant.
- Loses meaning or changes: the test above about a read held behind a held write; `time_read_wait_by_*` (a read no longer waits behind the worker's activities: the reader's own spans replace them, and the status file gets a new version); my timing tests that hold a step to make a read wait (`a_read_that_arrives_during_a_step_...`, `a_read_behind_a_step_and_two_writes_...`, `a_read_behind_a_write_that_makes_room_...`, `an_explicit_checkpoints_parts_...`, `a_completion_taken_by_wait_...`) would need a write or a flush as the waiting request. `READ_HOME_ONLY`, `INDEX_SWITCH_EARLY` and `READ_ERROR_SERVED` keep their meaning.
- Kernel: the daemon rig's check c (the conformance suite through the device) covers reads racing writes only as far as its load makes them race. A load with reads of blocks being rewritten while steps run would be new. Unsure which of the five WAITING pins of M1.T8 touch this.

**Option 3.**
- The states on the disk are not new in kind: a batch is already a prefix of the log with later records present, so "the home image half applied, the log longer than the batch" exists today. What is new is that appends, log syncs and rolls happen *between* the step's writes. The simulated disk orders all writes of one thread by one seed, and the cut tests enumerate cuts in that order. With two threads writing, either the step's thread is run by the simulated disk at chosen points (an explicit schedule, part of the seed), or the tests stop being reproducible. Building that schedule and re-proving the cut tests over it is the larger part of option 3, larger than the product code.
- New, to be shown: a cut at every point of a step with appends, syncs and rolls interleaved at every point; a roll while the step's thread reads the segment being closed; the log failing while a step is between its writes (the step stops before its next write); the step failing while the worker appends (nothing acknowledged depended on it); the bound of the log with appends held at the high mark.
- Loses meaning or changes: the step hooks no longer hold the worker, so every test that uses a held step to hold the worker changes (the timing tests above, and the rule-of-the-marks tests that assert what the worker does between steps); the gate (`steps_first`, `time_gate_*`, the bound "high mark plus one request" of #19) becomes "appends held while a step runs at the high mark"; the worker's fourteen activities no longer add up to the time served with the step's parts in them (the step's parts are another thread's time: a new status version); `REFUSAL_ENDS_NO_STEP` and the mutants on the order inside a step keep their meaning. The daemon rig's check of a step under `kill -9` keeps its meaning and gets a new case (a kill with appends during the step).

## 7. Size, what is most likely to go wrong, and the order

All sizes are guesses from the code's shape, product code without tests; the tests are at least as much again, for option 3 more.

| | size | most likely to go wrong |
|---|---|---|
| 4B, one reader thread | 400 to 600 lines: the index behind a lock, the reader, the pin and its two barriers, resolving a log position to a handle without the log's start, the failure path, the timing | A path that reuses or changes a file without passing a barrier (a third place I have not seen: `make_room`, the explicit checkpoint and a refused deallocation's early end all go through `apply`, so I believe there are only the two). A reader's failure not marking the log before its completion goes out |
| 4A, the pump reads | 250 to 350 lines | The stall on a miss; invisible in every test we have |
| 4C, the ring | 1000 lines or more, across three crates, and a contract change | The pin held across asynchronous reads; a request of several extents completing in parts; the conformance suite no longer on the real path |
| 3a, the step's thread, start on the worker | 500 to 800 lines, plus the simulated disk's schedule | The test model: cuts between two threads' writes. In the product: splitting `Log` so that a second thread can read records while the first appends and rolls; the appends' hold at the high mark racing the step's report |

**Order I would build in.** 4B with one reader first: it removes the largest part of a read's time (the step and everything else on the worker, 153 to 169 µs of wait by my sum), it leaves the disk's order and every durability argument alone, and its new guards are two barriers that hooks can test directly. Then read the numbers again: if the writes' or flushes' waits behind a step matter for the criterion (synchronous commits), 3a is the answer for those and its design is not made harder by 4B (the step's thread takes over the barriers from the worker). If the two hand-overs are what is left (64 µs of about 120), the choice is between several readers that spin briefly instead of sleeping, which is cheap and costs a core, and 4C.

What the fast-runner numbers could change in this: if on fast machines the step's share of a read's wait falls and the hand-overs' share rises (the lead's observation that the gap grows where the machine is faster points that way), 4B's gain shrinks towards the 64 µs it does not touch, and shape A or C, or a reader that spins, becomes the question.

## Not asked: two smaller things the numbers point at

- **Smaller steps.** A read waits behind a step roughly in proportion to how often a blocking call happens times the square of how long it is. A step is 16.2 ms about 2.3 times a second. The step's bound is a parameter (`Marks::step`); a quarter of the payload per step, four times as often, would cut the apply's part of the wait and leave the per-step syncs (home 3.68, start 2.71, own 1.08 ms), which do not shrink with the batch and would then happen four times as often. So this helps less than it looks and may cost throughput; it is a run of the benchmark with another parameter, not code.
- **One walk instead of two.** The walk that chooses the batch is 3.05 ms of a step's 16.2 on the kernel (a fifth), and the apply then reads the same records again. Choosing the batch while applying would remove it. Small, and inside the step, so it changes no order; it helps every option.
