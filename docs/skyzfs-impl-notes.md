## How to read these notes

- **The design says what and why. These notes say how it was built and what was found.** A reader new to the project starts with the design.
- **The order is milestone, then work package, then component.** Each milestone has its status, a table of its work packages with their pull requests, and then the detail, under the heading of the design section it belongs to.
- **"§4.2" names a section of the design.** Text that stood in that section until 2026-11-12 is here under the heading that carries its number, for example "The ublk transport (design §4.2)". Where a paragraph says "§9" for a measurement or a decision, the full account is here under "The throughput criterion (design §9)".
- **The paragraphs are kept as they were written**, while the work was done: dated, dense, and with the run or the pull request each number rests on. "Above" and "below" in them refer to their neighbours in the same section.
- How the rigs and the benchmark are run is in `CLAUDE.md`, "Easy to miss".

## M1: Linux prototype

Deliverables and exit criteria: design §9.

### Status

M1 is in progress: the contract of §4.1 is merged as an executable model with a conformance suite that reports what a given harness cannot exercise (`crates/skyzfs-blk`), so are the write log and the store on top of it, tested on a simulated disk and, under `kill -9`, on real files (`crates/skyzfs-local`, §5.1), and so is the power-cut replay rig that M1's exit criterion rests on, which reads every copy of every reachable block and checks what `fsync` promised, first against the ublk project's reference server (`tests/kernel/replay-rig.sh`) and since M1.T14 through `skyzfsd` with the cut below it (`tests/kernel/replay_daemon.py`), where it has shown the criterion. A second kernel rig takes the daemon, and a stand-in for it, through kills, an orderly stop, a held request, a failed volume, the conformance suite and its checkpoints (`tests/kernel/daemon-rig.sh`). The ublk transport and `skyzfsd` are built and run against the kernel on Ubuntu 24.04 and 26.04 (`crates/skyzfs-ublk`, `crates/skyzfs-ublk-sys`, §4.2). What M1 still lacks is its throughput criterion (§9), and one thing this sentence left out until the review of 2026-11-12: the replay rig has not been run on `skyzfsd` itself, so the power-cut criterion is shown for the rig and the reference server and not yet for the daemon's log.

### The contract (design §4.1)

The rules are in the design. What follows is the detail that the design leaves out: the corner cases of a failed volume, what the suite's harness capabilities cost, and what the deployment check sees.

**A failed volume, in full.**

- The write log has failed for good once a write or a sync of it has failed. From then on the volume has **failed**: no flush the daemon receives succeeds, and from the first failure the daemon reports, **every later request fails**, reads included. A request already in flight when the log fails may still complete either way. A flush among them succeeds only if its work was done before the failure. A disk that would refuse the next sync, and has not been asked, has not failed yet: a flush that finds every acknowledged operation already covered by a completed flush has nothing to make durable, promises nothing new, and may succeed without asking the disk. The daemon is not required to probe. Every completed flush stays a promise on a failed volume, whichever side of the failure it completed on: when the volume is next opened for a new client, after a power cut or a repair, it holds a prefix of the daemon's order that includes at least what each of them covered, exactly as after a power cut. Recovery is one procedure, and a cut is a position in that order (§5.2), so no recovery may leave anything but a prefix. A daemon restart in between adds nothing to that promise: what an instance acknowledged on a log that has failed, and no completed flush covered, may be missing when the volume is opened again. The rule that an acknowledged operation survives the daemon holds for a log that has not failed. While the volume is failed, nothing else about its content is promised. ZFS ignores the result of the flushes on either side of its uberblocks[^zfserr], so it is the next write that has to fail, and the content of a block whose log record was lost is unknown, so a read of it must not return something older.
- A failed volume stays failed across a daemon restart for as long as its client stays attached. A new instance must never serve a shorter log to a ZFS that was told more. Three things keep that rule when the log itself is what failed: the log leaves a marker (§5.1), the transport gives up the attachment so that no later instance can recover it, and every start in the same boot checks that the log it recovered reaches everything acknowledged in that boot (§4.2). A failed volume fails every request, including the reads and writes with which ZFS probes a device that has just returned an error. That is what tells ZFS the device is gone: when its probe fails too, ZFS itself raises `ENXIO`, the one error on which it suspends the pool and does not pass a read failure to an application[^readerr]. The kernel rig has seen both: the pool suspended, and its readers and writers blocked without an error (§4.2). A transport need not deliver `ENXIO`, and on Linux it cannot (§4.2). It must not answer with an error that means something else to ZFS, above all "not supported".

**The conformance suite and its harness.**

The suite drives an implementation through a harness, and a harness states what it cannot do: cut the power, start from a base that is not empty, serve a read-only volume, fail the log on demand, issue an invalid request, reopen a failed volume. A case whose property needs a missing capability is reported as not exercised, with the capability named, before it runs anything. A case that used the capability only to check more runs in a weaker form and says which. Nothing is left out silently, and a harness must not claim a capability it only imitates. This is what lets one suite run both in process and through a kernel block device, where a rig can do few of these things. The cost is stated with it: a harness with none of the six sees 13 of the 26 cases not exercised and 12 weakened, and 15 of the model's 24 planted faults pass it unnoticed, among them every fault that only a power cut reveals. So "the suite passes through `/dev/ublkbN`" is a much weaker statement than "the suite passes in process", and the durability of the whole stack rests on the store's own tests and on the replay rig (§9), not on that run.

**The two deployment rules.**

Two deployment rules are outside the suite and are covered by a stress test in the kernel rig: the daemon locks its memory, and it keeps its own files off any pool it serves. A daemon that page-faults onto its own pool deadlocks the machine. `skyzfsd serve` checks both before it touches a device. For the second it refuses a volume directory or a run directory on any file system of type `zfs`, whichever pool that is, because it cannot know which pools its devices will carry. That is wider than the rule in one way and narrower in another: a file system of another type that itself lies on ZFS, on a zvol or on a loop file, is not seen, and keeping the daemon's files off those stays the operator's rule. Each check has an override, for tests.

### The ublk transport (design §4.2)

What the device must do (recovery that reissues, flushes that reach the daemon, requests split by the kernel, requests that may be held) is in the design. What follows is how the daemon keeps and gives up its device, what was learned from the driver, and what the rigs have shown.

#### One instance per volume

- **One instance per volume.** The store takes an exclusive lock on the volume's directory when it is opened and holds it until the process ends. Opening a store is a recovery that writes, so a second daemon must be stopped before it opens anything. The lock belongs to the open file and not to the process: a child process holds a copy from its fork until its exec, and the lock outlives a plain close for that long. So the store unlocks explicitly when it closes, and the daemon starts no other process while it serves. If a daemon is killed while a child of its own is between the two, the next instance may find the volume locked for a moment and must try again. The kernel also refuses a second server for a device: beginning a recovery is refused as busy while the device is live, or while a dying server has not yet let go of it[^ublkdrv]. But the daemon reaches that step only after the store is open, so the refusal is no substitute for the lock. **Everything that changes a volume's attachment is done under this lock**: its record, its notes, adding and deleting its device. That holds for a start that fails as much as for one that serves, and for `skyzfsd detach`. Taking the lock is therefore a step of its own before the recovery: the lock first, then the acknowledged position is read, then the store is recovered against it. Read before the lock, the position could be older than what an instance that was still serving went on to acknowledge.

#### Attachment across restarts

- **Attachment across restarts.** Before it adds a device the daemon records, durably and in the volume's directory, the device's number, a fresh random nonce, the kernel's boot id, and the size, queues, depth and flags it will ask for. It asks the driver which numbers are taken and passes those over before it records one. If the kernel still refuses the number, the daemon chooses another and writes the record again before it adds. Its note for the device under `/run`, with the same nonce, is written only once the add has succeeded, and before the device is started. **A note exists only while its device may exist**: wherever the daemon or `skyzfsd detach` deletes a device, the note is removed first. A note that outlived its device would vouch for whatever program was given the freed number next. The kernel keeps no identity for a device, so whose device stands under the recorded number is the daemon's bookkeeping, and only as good as the files it rests on. With a record from this boot it is decided by the note first and by the kernel's values second:
  - A whole note with the record's nonce: the device is this volume's. If it waits for a server it is taken over, provided the size, queues, depth and flags the kernel reports are the recorded ones; if they are not, the daemon stops and touches nothing. If it was added and never started it is deleted, and a new one is added. If it is still live the daemon asks again for up to 5 s and refuses if it stays live: after a `kill -9` the kernel goes on reporting the device as live, with no server, for a moment. Timed over 20 kills per image in each of four runs[^liverig]: on Ubuntu 24.04 the device was still live 209 ms after the kill in one run (158, 54 and 6 ms at most in the others) and no longer live by 222 ms at the latest; on 26.04 no look found it live in three runs, and in the fourth it was still live after 56 ms at most and no longer live by 76 ms. Why some kills take that long was not looked into. The next instance can hold the store's lock well before that.
  - A whole note with another nonce: another volume's device, which got the number between this volume's look and its add. The daemon leaves it and attaches under another number.
  - No note, and the kernel's values differ from the record: not this volume's, because a device is added with exactly the recorded values and they cannot change afterwards[^ublkdrv]. The daemon leaves it and attaches under another number.
  - No note, and the same values: the daemon cannot tell this volume's device after a lost `/run` from a stranger's of the same geometry. A device that was never started has never had a client, so it is left and the daemon attaches elsewhere. That leaks one number for every daemon killed between its add and its note, until an operator deletes the device or the machine reboots. A live or waiting device may have this volume's client on it, so the daemon stops, touches nothing, and names the device for an operator to judge.
  - A note that is not a whole record: the volume is treated as failed (below) and the device is left.
  - A device that was deleted while a client held it keeps its number until the client lets go (below), and the driver reports it as dead, exactly as it reports one that was added and never started. The two are not told apart. A dead device with this volume's note is deleted, which changes nothing if it was deleted before, and the daemon attaches under another number; one without a note is left. A dead device is never taken over, and an add passes its number by.

  After a reboot no client can still be attached, and the daemon adds a new device. A device's parameters cannot be changed once it has been started[^ublkdrv], so a new instance follows the device's values and not its own defaults. The flags the kernel reports include bits the daemon did not ask for, so they are compared under the mask of what it asked (Library, below). At every start the store is recovered and made durable before a recovery is begun or a device goes live (§4.1). The kernel delivers no request before the recovery is ended.

#### The states of a device

- **The states of a device.** The driver reports three states: dead, live, and waiting for a server. With the flags the daemon asks for (recovery by the user, with reissue), these are the events and where each leads. The last column says what the row rests on: the spike[^spike], the binding's own kernel tests[^bind], the daemon rig or the failed-volume observations[^failrig][^liverig], or a reading of the driver[^ublkdrv] that no run has shown yet.

  | The device is | Event | It becomes | Rests on |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | not there | an add under a free number | dead: it has a number, a control node and no block device | spike, binding's tests |
  | in any state | an add under its number | unchanged; the add is refused as taken | spike, for a device that was added and for one deleted and held; binding's tests for one that was added; reading for the rest |
  | dead, never started | its parameters are set, its queue fetches, it is started | live | spike, binding's tests |
  | dead, never started | the process that added it ends | unchanged | spike |
  | dead, never started | a process begins a recovery | unchanged; the recovery is refused as busy, the same answer as for a server that is still being torn down | binding's tests, one run per image |
  | live | its server ends, by a kill or by an orderly stop, which never stops the device | waiting, after a moment in which it still reads live (above). Requests in flight and new ones are held, without error | daemon rig and spike for a kill; spike and binding's tests for a server that ends without a kill, since the rig's stand-in server stops by being killed |
  | live | another process begins a recovery | unchanged; the recovery is refused as busy | reading |
  | waiting | a server begins a recovery, fetches, and ends the recovery | live. No request is delivered before the end; then the requests that were in flight are issued again, and none fails | daemon rig, spike, binding's tests |
  | waiting | a server begins a recovery and ends before ending it | unchanged; the next server can take over | spike, one run per image |
  | waiting | no server comes | unchanged, with no limit in time: the driver never times a request out for a device added by root. Seen for 300 s under a pool: the device read as waiting at every look, the kernel logged no I/O error, no request timed out and no abort, and the take-over afterwards lost nothing. After about two minutes the kernel reports every task blocked on the pool as hung (the pool's sync thread, a `zpool sync`, a writer); those are warnings and nothing fails | reading for the limit; daemon rig for 300 s, three runs per image[^waitrig] |
  | in any state, with nothing holding the block device open | a delete | not there; the number is free | spike; binding's tests; daemon rig for a waiting device |
  | in any state, held open | a delete | dead, with no nodes; every request in flight and every later one fails; the number stays taken. Nothing under `/dev` or `/sys` names the device any more, so a look through those does not show it and only a lookup of its number does. When the holder lets go the device is not there | failed-volume observations, spike |
  | dead after a delete | anything but its holder letting go | unchanged: it cannot be started, taken over or added over | spike for the add; reading for the rest |

  The daemon sends no command that stops a device without deleting it, so that event is not in the table. A dead device that was never started and one that was deleted while held differ in their nodes and in nothing the driver reports; the rules above do not need to tell them apart.

#### A failed volume gives up its attachment

- **A failed volume gives up its attachment.** When the store fails (the write log, or with the in-place store a write or sync of the image; the note's reason says which, codes 1 and 7, M1.3x), the daemon writes a failure note under the host's `/run`, answers every request it holds with an error, removes the device's note, deletes the ublk device, and exits. It does not wait for the log's own marker (§5.1), which needs the disk that has just failed and may never be written. The delete is the asynchronous one, and nothing waits for the number to come free. The driver frees a number only when the last holder of the deleted device lets go, the plain delete waits for exactly that, and a suspended pool never lets go[^ublkdrv]. A daemon that waited would hang on its way out, holding the store's lock. Deleting the device fails every request that arrives afterwards, and ZFS finds the disk dead when it checks[^ublkdrv]. A request that the server holds is another matter, and this paragraph was wrong about it until a rig measured it: the driver's delete of a live device, the asynchronous one included, does not return until the server has answered every request it holds, the held request does not fail meanwhile, and for as long as the delete waits every add and every delete of any ublk device on the node waits behind it[^delwait]. If the server's process ends, the waiting delete returns at once and the held request fails with an I/O error. So a failed volume must hold no request when its device is deleted: the daemon answers every request it holds with an error, hands the store nothing more, and only then deletes. That is built (M1.3d, skyzfs/skyzfs#18). At the failure the queue's thread answers every request the daemon holds with an I/O error, whether it was not yet handed to the store, in the store, behind its flush, or completed and waiting for the acknowledged position. An I/O error promises nothing, so it is always a permitted answer; the write may still be in the log. From then on the daemon hands the store nothing, takes nothing from it, stores no position, and answers what still arrives with the same error. The main thread writes the failure note first, waits up to 2 s for the queue's thread to say that it has answered, and then deletes the device whatever happened, and its message says which. If the driver refuses the delete, the device's note is put back, so that the next start or `detach` finds the device and deletes it. Run on a kernel with the daemon, twelve failures over two runs on each of two images: 1 to 86 requests were held at the failure, all were answered before the delete, the daemon exited with the failure's status, and the device was gone[^failheld]. How long those deletes took, and that no other device's add or delete waited meanwhile, is not measured yet (M1.T8).

  Two waits are left without a bound. A store call that hangs and never fails sets no failure: its requests stay held and an orderly stop waits with them (§10). And at a failure, if the queue's thread is itself stuck in the one call it makes that can hang, the write of the acknowledged position, it answers nothing; the delete is sent after the 2 s all the same and waits in the driver as measured below, with every add and delete on the node behind it, and the daemon says so before it sends it. A known failure makes that record refuse, so only a write that began before the failure can hang there. After the delete the process itself may linger: the store's worker can be inside a write on the failed disk, the process does not end until that returns, and it keeps the volume's lock until then, on purpose, because a worker that may still write must keep another instance out.

  **Whoever holds the volume's lock and finds the failed volume's device waiting for a server deletes it:** the start that refuses a held failure, the start that refuses an unheld failure for want of an acknowledgement, the start that acknowledges, and `detach`. A refusing start changes nothing else, the failure note, the acknowledged position and the log's marker stay as they were, and it says what became of the device. Until M1.3d the start that refused an unheld failure touched nothing at all, and a daemon killed between its note and its delete left a device that held whatever was sent to it, without limit and without a word. That case, a device left waiting by a daemon killed before its delete, is in the unit tests and has not run on a kernel. That a kill ends a delete's wait is measured with a test server in another process than the one that deletes. Where the daemon completes a request of a failed volume itself, it answers with a plain I/O error: the block layer carries a status and not an errno, and no status reaches ZFS as `ENXIO`[^ublkdrv].
  `/run` is kept in memory and emptied by a reboot, which is exactly how long the note has to live. The note also carries the boot id, for a `/run` that is not. It says whether a client held the device open at the failure: yes, no, or not known, because only an exclusive holder can be detected without I/O (Library, below). A later start settles "not known" by the driver, before anything else: if the device that the volume's record names is no longer there, nobody holds it, and the note is rewritten as not held. In every other case it counts as held: the device is still there, this boot has no record of a device for the volume, the record or the device's note cannot be read, or the settled value cannot be written. Every start reads the note before it takes a device over or adds one. If a client held the device, the volume is not served again until the next boot, whatever an operator says. The reason is `zpool clear`: a suspended pool's status tells the operator to bring the device back and clear the error, and on a device that is served again ZFS then carries on from its own memory, on a log that may be shorter than what it was told. That is measured, below. It matters whenever the daemon dies before it has deleted the device, which then waits for a server like the device of a daemon that was only killed. If no client held the device, an operator may acknowledge the failure and serve the volume again. In another boot the store checks the log from the device before it reopens (§5.1).
  What the kernel rig has measured of this, on Linux 7.0 with ZFS 2.4.1 and zed not running, with a stand-in server and not yet `skyzfsd`, in one run on each of two images[^failrig]:
  - When every request is answered with an error, or the device is deleted, the pool is suspended, within a few seconds where that was timed (the deleted device). Readers and writers block, and none is handed an error. The vdev is still shown as online, with errors counted.
  - **That holds when a read or a flush fails. A pool that only writes does not suspend: ZFS retries its failed writes without end.** Measured on the daemon, in four runs of the kernel rig, one per image each[^refwr]: a pool under a writer of 4 MiB files with `fsync`, the daemon killed, and the next start refused, which deletes the waiting device. Nothing was waiting at the device then: the writer's data was in the intent log and in memory. From the first look after the refusal the device had no node. The transaction group that began to sync a few seconds later never finished: the pool's list of transaction groups was the same at 10 s, at 60 s and at the end, and the syncing thread stood in the same wait. The pool and the vdev read ONLINE throughout, while the vdev's count of write errors ran into the tens of millions and the pool posted error events by the ten thousand a second, 14,000 to 27,000 by their numbering, every one a write that failed with `ENXIO` and was executed again. No event said that the device was gone or had failed a probe. That is how ZFS is written: a write that needs an allocation and may not fail is executed again for every error but a full pool, and only a read or a free that fails, or a write with nowhere left to go, suspends the pool[^zioreexec]. With writes alone the pool did not suspend in any of eight courses on the two images, for up to 351 s. The first read that had to reach the device suspended it within a second or two: a read of a file that was in no cache, in four of four, and the start of a scrub, in two of two. Meanwhile synchronous writes went on being acknowledged out of the log device (§6.1): a second writer's 4 MiB write with `fsync` returned in 132 to 159 ms, minutes after the device was gone, in five of six tries. In the sixth it hung, in the one job where the first writer had by then been told "written" for 192 MB more. A pool with no log device did not acknowledge: the same write hung, two of two. Earlier runs with the pool at rest gave the same course in small: one failed write, and the pool ONLINE two minutes later. What an operator sees while it lasts: `zpool status` says ONLINE, with a status line about data corruption, the vdev ONLINE with write errors in the millions, and a file named as having permanent errors; the kernel log has a few lines at the first second and then nothing until the hung-task reports two minutes later. So "ZFS finds the disk dead when it checks" is true only of reads, and a pool that only writes does not check. For the product this is decision D2's loss without its bound: applications are told "written" for data that is only on the local log device and in memory, for as long as the limit on dirty data allows, a core is spent on the retries and the events, and nothing says that the volume is gone. **The rule that follows: after a failure or a refusal, the pool of an attached client is made to read, so that it suspends within a bound.** The daemon cannot do that: it is below ZFS, the device is gone, and it knows no pool. It says so in every message of a failure or a refusal and in `skyzfsd status`, which reports with no daemon running what became of a failed volume's device and warns about a pool on it (M1.3d, built), and the agent, which owns the pool, acts on it: when the daemon reports a volume failed or refused under an imported pool, the agent starts a read that must reach the device and reports the pool as lost for this boot (M2). Until the agent exists that is the operator's step, and the daemon's message names it. Measured since, by the daemon rig's check f (by dispatch, two clean runs per course and image, on a pool with a log device and `failmode=wait`[^checkf]): a scrub and a `zpool reopen` both do it. Either makes the pool suspend about 1.0 to 1.1 s after the command and let go of the device: the vdev reads UNAVAIL, the driver forgets the number, and an add under it succeeds. The reopen returns, with status 0, in about a second; the scrub command does not return. `zpool export` and `zpool clear` do neither: the command does not return, the pool does not suspend within 200 s, and the device stays held. Later synced writers alone may return or may hang (in one run of four all nine hung, with 194 MiB of dirty data), so nothing may rely on either. **So the agent's step is a reopen, with a limit on the command and a look at the pool's state afterwards, since the command's return is not the signal.** zed changes this on Ubuntu 26.04 (ZFS 2.4.1): there the pool suspends by itself before any command, keeps the device, and refuses both a scrub and a reopen ("pool I/O is currently suspended"), in two runs each. On 24.04 with zed a reopen still freed the device in two runs of two and returned in one of them. So where zed runs as on 26.04, no command that was tried frees the device, and what the agent does there is open: the question for M2 is whether to give such a pool a device that answers rather than to free the one it holds. Not yet measured: whether the same holds after a failure while serving, where the daemon answers what it holds with an error before it deletes.
  - A delete of a live device whose server holds a request waits for that request. Measured with a test server and no pool, in one run on each image, the two agreeing to about 100 ms[^delwait]: with a read held for 20 s and the delete sent 2 s into the hold, the delete returned after 18.0 s, and after 58.0 s of a hold of 60 s; the held read then returned its data. While the delete waited, an add under an unrelated number and a delete of a second, idle device each waited 17 s and returned when it did, and so did a lookup of the device itself that reads its parameters; a lookup of a free number was answered in about 0.1 s. When the server was killed 5 s after the delete was sent, the delete returned within 20 ms of the kill, the commands that had waited returned with it, and the held read failed with an I/O error. With a client holding the device open and the server holding nothing, which is the case the paragraph above was written for, the delete returned in 11 to 21 ms over two runs, the client's next read failed, and the number came free 22 ms after the client let go, measured once, on 26.04, in an earlier run of the same checks.
  - On a device that was kept and is served again, the pool stays suspended until `zpool clear`. After it the pool is online, the blocked writers carry on, and no error is counted. ZFS has resumed from its memory, which is why a failed volume must not be served to it again. What that costs was then seen: the kept device was served again from an older copy of its image, `zpool clear` brought the pool online, and ZFS carried on with hundreds of checksum errors and with files lost or damaged that it had acknowledged after an `fsync`, 7 and 11 of the writer's 16 in two runs on one image, 1 and 14 of 16 on the other. The copy was taken from a live image, so it is an earlier and possibly torn state and not a clean one; the outcome is what a shorter log would give.
  - On a device that was deleted, nothing that was tried brought the pool back: not a new device under another number, and not one with the old path pointing at it. The old number cannot be had while the pool holds the deleted device. `zpool clear` on such a pool did not return and could not be killed, while `zpool status` still answered.
  - So deleting the device is final for that import, and nothing short of a reboot was found that removes the suspended pool. `zpool export -f` and `zpool destroy -f` both failed at once and left it listed and suspended, on a pool on which a `zpool clear` was already stuck and equally on one that nothing had touched since it suspended[^liverig], and the device's number stayed taken afterwards. A scrub that was running when the device failed stays reported as in progress. Unmounting its datasets first (`umount -l`) changed nothing: both commands then answered that the pool's I/O is suspended. A failed write log under an imported pool therefore costs a reboot of the node before that pool can be imported again. Other pools on the node keep running meanwhile.
  - The design does not lean on ZFS declining to resume. Why the pool did not take the device behind the old path is not established, so the daemon's own rule stands: not served again in this boot.
  - A suspended pool produced between 0.4 and 2.7 million pool events per scenario, in every run but one. Whatever reads events on the node has to survive that. With zed running nothing else changed: the pool stayed suspended and `zpool clear` blocked. zed itself logged a missed event some fourteen thousand times in thirty seconds and took a tenth of a CPU.
  - A suspended pool does not hold up the syncing of other file systems: beside one, `fsync` and `fdatasync` of a file on the root file system and `syncfs` of that file system returned as fast as before. What did not return was the `sync FILE` command of Ubuntu 26.04, which is the Rust rewrite of coreutils and not GNU's; GNU's on 24.04 returned at once. A `sync()` of everything did not return either, on both images, while `fsync` and `syncfs` of the other file system went on returning beside it. What 26.04's command calls was not traced; it behaves like the latter. **The daemon never syncs anything but its own files and directories**, and neither do its scripts: one failed pool must not stall the other volumes on the node.

  Two limits. A daemon in a container must be given the host's `/run`, or a restarted container forgets the note. If neither the note nor the marker can be written, a later instance cannot know that the volume failed; in the same boot the check below still catches a log that came back shorter.

#### The run directory, and detaching

- **The run directory is shared by every daemon on the machine**, so nothing in it is one file per directory. A device's note is named by the device's number, which is unique on the machine. Everything that belongs to a volume is in a subdirectory named by the device and inode numbers of the volume's directory: the acknowledged position (below), the failure note, and a small status file. The serving daemon replaces the status file as its counters change, so that `skyzfsd status` reports without opening the store and without a channel to the daemon; writing it never holds or fails a request. Inode numbers can be reused within a boot, which is why the store's identity is inside the records where a mix-up would matter.
- **Detaching.** `skyzfsd detach` deletes the device of a volume whose daemon has stopped. It never stops a daemon, and it refuses a device with a live server. It holds the volume's lock from its first look to its last step, without recovering the store, and refuses if a daemon has it: otherwise a daemon that starts in between has its device deleted under it, or its fresh record removed. It removes the note, deletes asynchronously, looks for the deleted device for up to 5 s, and removes the attachment record. The driver's delete returns before an unheld device is gone: on the runners that took up to about 30 ms, and the device was still there at the first look once in several hundred deletes[^m13b]. So only a device that outlasts the looks counts as held. The looks ask about the deleted device and not about its number, which another volume's start can take as soon as it is free: a device there with other values, with a note, or with a live server is somebody else's, and the deleted one is gone. A device with the same values, no note and no server stays "held", the safe side, also where it is another volume's whose start was killed between its add and its note. A start that settles a failure of unknown holding looks the same way. If a client still holds the device `detach` says so, with an exit status of its own (6; 0 only when nothing of the volume is left attached, 1 when it cannot tell whose the device is): by the measurements above that client's pool is cut off and will not resume in this boot, so a pool is exported before its volume is detached. `detach` cannot know beforehand that no client holds a waiting device: only an exclusive holder can be detected without I/O (Library, below). So it does not ask first. It deletes, looks, and says what it found.

#### What was acknowledged is checked at every start

- **What was acknowledged is checked at every start.** *(As built for the log store, M1.2. The check went with the log (decision D5), and the record of the acknowledged position, its pump and lifecycle code and the file `acknowledged` were removed in M1.3t-b, skyzfs/skyzfs#74. The start line still says `position N ms` and `recovered_unacknowledged`, which the replay rig parses; the step times the failure note's read alone.)* The failure note is written when the daemon learns of the failure, and a daemon that dies first leaves none. That is not a narrow window. A write-back error reaches the daemon only at its next sync. If the daemon is gone by then, the kernel reports the error once to whoever syncs the file next, and forgets it, together with the pages it could not write, if the file's inode leaves memory first[^wberr]. The next instance would then recover a shorter log without any sign of it, complete the flush that the kernel issues again, and ZFS would carry on believing in writes that are gone. So the daemon keeps, in its record for the volume under `/run`, the log position at which the last record it has acknowledged starts.
  - The position is stored before the request is completed to the kernel, on every queue, and it never moves back: one writer, or a comparison before every store. It is overwritten in place at a fixed width, so that a daemon killed in the act leaves the old value or the new one. If it cannot be stored, the request is not acknowledged. It is held, like any condition that can recover (§4.1). An orderly stop does not wait for that without limit: it tries a stated number of times, then ends without completing those requests and says that it was not an orderly stop. The kernel gives them to the next server again.
  - Every start in the same boot compares, before a device is taken over or added: the prefix that recovery found valid must end beyond the stored position. Not the log's end: recovery seals the prefix with a mark behind it, so the log always ends beyond the place where a lost record began. For the same reason the recovery makes the comparison itself, before it writes the seal or anything else. A log that fails it is left as it was, and a daemon that dies at that moment leaves the next one to find the same. Positions are compared, not records. After a checkpoint the record may be in the home image and no longer in the log; its position is then below the log's start, and the comparison still holds. Storing where the record starts keeps the comparison clear of how a record's end is padded at a change of segment.
  - The position belongs to the volume and the boot, not to one device. It survives a take-over and a detach followed by a new attachment, because `zpool clear` resumes a suspended pool on a device that is served again (measured, above). It is stored with an identity of the store it belongs to, derived from the log's key: a volume made again under the same name, or rolled back to a cut, is another store with a log that rightly ends lower, and a position recorded for another store counts as none. The store makes that comparison too, inside its open, because the identity is known only once the log has been read. It starts empty in a new boot, and again when an operator has acknowledged a failure of a volume that no client held.
  - A daemon that was only killed always passes: an acknowledged record was whole in the kernel's cache before its position was stored, and recovery makes the cache durable and then validates what the device holds. The check rests on that last step (§5.1). The pages that could not be written stay readable through the cache for a while, so a recovery that validated through the cache would pass and lose the data later. A platform with no read path around the cache cannot rely on this check.
  - A start also counts the operations it recovered beyond the stored position: what the server before it had applied and not yet acknowledged, which the kernel then issues again. `skyzfsd status` shows the count. It is a lower bound. The position is stored before the answers of a batch go to the kernel, so a kill between the two leaves requests unanswered that the count does not include, and an operation that a checkpoint has already moved out of the log is not counted either.
  - If the comparison fails, or if the recovery's own sync returns the error, acknowledged operations are gone and a client may still be attached. The volume is treated as one that failed while held: the note is written, this volume's device is deleted if it waits for a server or was never started, and the volume is not served again in this boot. An error from the recovery's sync is never "could not open, try again".

  A false alarm suspends a healthy pool, so the kernel rig is to pin that kills under load never raise one, across a change of log segment and right after a checkpoint that reclaimed the whole log. How the kernel loses the pages is read from its history and not measured here.

#### The library, pinned memory and copies

- **Library.** The transport is built on the `libublk` crate[^libublk], pinned to one version and confined to one module, for the queue: fetching requests, the per-request buffers, committing results, and waking the queue's thread from another one. It is written by the maintainer of the kernel driver. A spike against the kernel settled what a reading of the source had left open[^spike]. A request can be executed on a worker thread and completed on the queue's own thread after an eventfd wake: a verified load ran clean, and three kills per image under it were each taken over with reissue and no error. That was the condition for building on it. The control path is used only in part. In this version a failed control command comes back as "not supported" whatever the driver answered, and a failed add deletes the device that holds the number, with the delete that waits, because the object is dropped inside its constructor. The daemon's start depends on "this number is taken" being an answer, and must never delete a stranger's device. So the daemon sends these control commands itself and takes the driver's own answers: add, lookup of a device's state and of its parameters, the asynchronous delete, and the begin and the end of a recovery. The begin of a recovery is answered as busy while the server before is still being torn down, and the daemon asks again for up to five seconds. The library's control object is made with no flag and only for a device that exists, so dropping it deletes nothing. It sets the parameters and starts a device that this instance added, and it is the handle the queue is built on. Made that way the library writes no file: the daemon writes and removes nothing under `/run/ublksrvd`, the directory of the ublk project's own server, and a file of another server found there under the same number is left as it was. The binding's own tests showed each of these against the kernel[^bind]. What the spike also fixed: the driver reports three states and not four, since a device that was never started and one that was deleted while held both read as dead, so the rules above treat a dead device by its note alone; the driver reports more flag bits than were asked for, so flags are compared under the mask of what the daemon set; and only an exclusive holder of a device can be detected without I/O, so the daemon can know that a client holds its device but never that none does. A failure note therefore says held, not held, or unknown, and a later start settles an unknown by the driver: if the recorded device is no longer there, nobody holds it. All `unsafe` code of the transport is in one small crate beside the library. The costs: the build needs libclang, the crate brings some forty packages, and its API is still changing.
- **Pinned memory.** With one copy per request the daemon keeps two buffers per request slot, locked: the slot's own, and the one the library's first fetch of the slot names (Copies, below). That is 2 × queues × depth × largest request: at one queue, a depth of 64 and 1 MiB, 128 MiB for a volume, which matters with many pools on a node (M4). **These buffers are never given back to the allocator.** The driver holds their addresses in the fetch commands of the queue's thread for as long as that thread lives, also after the queue has been dropped at a stop, and it copies a write that arrives then to the address it was told. With the buffers freed at the drop, a run ended in a corrupted heap as soon as writes arrived after the memory had been handed out again[^m13b]. So the queue forgets its buffers instead of freeing them, and a buffer that is replaced is kept as well. A daemon serves once in its life, so this costs nothing there; a process that served several times would pay the buffers once per time. A test allocator that counts every deallocation inside the ranges the driver was told is what shows the property; damage is not waited for, because a test that waited for it passed with the defect in place. Gate G1 measures smaller values.
- **Copies.** One copy per request to start with. The buffer of a request slot is the daemon's own: the queue is told its address, the request carries the buffer to the store and the completion brings it back, so nothing is copied between the transport and the store. One exception: the library's first fetch of a request slot can name only a buffer of its own, so the first write of each slot after a start arrives there and is copied once more. A take-over is a start. That is at most the queue's depth in copies per start, and `skyzfsd status` counts them. The zero-copy modes are a later optimisation and are not needed to meet the study's numbers, which included a comparable per-request cost in dm-snapshot.

#### What the review of M1.3b added

- **What the review of M1.3b added to the rules above**[^m13b].
  - A request for which no buffer can be had is answered with an I/O error; nothing waits for memory on the request path.
  - A device whose largest request is larger than the daemon's buffers is not opened: the start fails as an error, before anything is served. The driver accepts such a device at the add, so the check is the daemon's.
  - An orderly stop hands the queue the last results it committed before the queue ends. If it cannot, the stop is an error and says how many completed requests were not answered; they are issued again to the next instance. A test that stops a server under writers 24 times fails when that step is taken out: 20 to 64 requests were then issued again after a stop that had committed them.
  - `create` makes the last part of its directory. It refuses a directory that holds anything but what a failed create leaves, by exact names, and a create that fails leaves nothing of a directory it made.
  - The volume's lock is on the file the name `lock` names. A lock taken on a file that was opened under that name and has since been unlinked or replaced excludes nobody, so after locking, the locked file is compared with what the name names, and the lock is taken again if they differ. That comparison is made on Unix only; whether the case arises on Windows is not known (a point for M7).
  - The main thread holds a share of the volume's lock for the whole of serving, whatever the queue's thread does with the store, so the cleanup after a failure runs under the lock also when that thread has panicked.
  - The rule that the daemon keeps its files off any pool (§4.1) is asked of the kernel, by `statfs` of the real path, and the mount table is not read. The volume's directory must exist and is judged as itself. The run directory is judged where it will be made and again once it is made, because a path can land elsewhere than predicted (`missing/../pool/run`). It is a guard against a deployment mistake and not a boundary against someone who can write there.
  - `ready` is printed only after the first write of the status file by this instance has been tried, for at most 5 s. `status` shows counters only from a status file that carries this store's identity.
  - "Gone to a lookup" comes before "free for an add" in the driver. After a delete, a lookup found no device and an add under the same number was answered "taken" in 5 of some 250 adds that followed a server's end, on both images (kernels 7.0.0-1017 and 7.0.0-1019), and in none of those that followed a device never started; the next add succeeded at most 31 ms after the delete. By the driver's source that is the time between the last reference going and its release having run, so nobody holds the device then, which is all the daemon takes from a lookup that finds nothing. The daemon's add treats "taken" as an answer and takes the next number.
  - Setting parameters on a started device is refused by the driver with a permission error, as the source said.

#### The ublk features the daemon needs, and the kernels that have them (M1.3q)

`crates/skyzfs-ublk/src/features.rs` is the one place that names them. They were read, on 2026-11-12, in `include/uapi/linux/ublk_cmd.h` at every release tag from v6.0 to v7.0 and in `drivers/block/ublk_drv.c` at ten of them, in the kernel's tree as mirrored on GitHub.

| What | First kernel | Why the daemon needs it |
|---|---|---|
| `UBLK_F_USER_RECOVERY` | 6.1 | A device waits for a server when its server ends |
| `UBLK_F_USER_RECOVERY_REISSUE` | 6.1 | Requests in flight at a server's end go to the next server |
| `UBLK_F_CMD_IOCTL_ENCODE` | 6.4 | The daemon's control commands are ioctl-encoded and are not tried again in the legacy encoding |
| `UBLK_U_CMD_GET_FEATURES` | 6.5 | How the start learns the three flags above before it adds a device |
| `UBLK_U_CMD_DEL_DEV_ASYNC` | 6.11 (named in the header from 6.9) | How a failed volume gives up its device while a client still holds it: the plain delete waits for the last holder, and a suspended pool never lets go |

**The oldest kernel is Linux 6.11.**

- **The header and the driver differ for the asynchronous delete.** The header names the command from v6.9. The drivers of v6.9 and v6.10 match control commands by the command's number and label this one's case with the whole ioctl code, which the number never equals, so the command falls to the default and is answered `ENOTSUPP` (errno 524). v6.11 defines the number and matches it. The lead's first decision was 6.9, from the header alone; the review read the driver, and the lead confirmed it in the source at the three tags.
- **What the start checks.** It sends the features command for a device number that is never a device's. A driver before 6.5 answers `ENODEV`, which is read as "the command is missing". The delete has no feature bit, and no command tells a driver that has it from one that lacks it without adding a device, so the start also reads the kernel's release and refuses one below 6.11 or one it cannot parse. `detach` checks the same once it holds the lock and has found a record of this boot; with no record it answers that there is nothing to detach and does not ask the driver. A refusal names each thing that is missing and the oldest kernel that has it.
- **The override.** `--kernel-has-async-delete`, given to `serve` and to `detach`, says that the driver executes the delete although the release is older: a distribution kernel with the driver backported, or a 6.9.y or 6.10.y that carries the fix. It skips the release check only; the features are still checked. A refused start offers it only when the release is all that is missing. A delete that the driver then answers `ENOTSUPP` is told to the operator as a command the driver does not know.
- **No fallback and no probe.** There is no fallback to the synchronous delete: it is the one that hangs under a suspended pool, which is the case the asynchronous one exists for. The review suggested probing instead of reading the release: add a device, delete it asynchronously, and see. Not taken: it adds and removes a device at every start, and a daemon killed between the two leaves a device nobody owns. It stays the alternative if release numbers prove a poor guide on distribution kernels.
- **Evidence.** The rigs run on kernel 7.0 on both images, so the refusals are shown by unit tests on the driver's answers as read in the source (features word, `ENODEV`, `ENOTSUPP`, releases with suffixes), not on an old kernel. No kernel between 6.11 and 7.0 has run the daemon.

### The local store (design §5.1)

The design has the store's parts and their purpose. What follows is each rule as built, in the order it was built.

#### The write log's format (M1.2a)

The write log itself is introduced in design §5.1. Its format:

The log's format rests on five rules. Each one came out of a test or a review that broke the simpler version.
- *Nothing below the log is atomic.* A record counts only if its header and its payload both verify. Recovery keeps the longest prefix of records that do, reads them from the device and not from the operating system's cache, and makes that prefix durable before the volume is served.
- *A block that holds flushed records is never written again.* The first record after a flush starts in a new write unit, and recovery marks the end of the prefix in a new unit. The unit is the size the layer below rewrites as a whole, stored in the log: 4 KiB on x86, 16 KiB on Apple silicon. Without this rule a torn rewrite of a shared block could take flushed records with it.
- *Record headers are keyed.* A record's payload is client data and can lie exactly where recovery looks for the next record. Headers therefore carry a 64-bit keyed tag chained to the record before, with a random key that is stored only in the log's own superblock. A client that only chooses what it writes cannot make a header. Whoever can read the log's files has the key, and could already write them, so the files are private to the daemon's user.
- *Corruption of what was durable is reported, never repaired and never read as a shorter log.* Every superblock write starts an epoch, so anything written after a lost superblock copy is recognisably newer and the open fails. A read error fails the open and changes nothing on the device. Such a volume falls back to its last uploaded cut, which D2 accepts through M4.
- *A failed write or flush fails the log for good*, which is the failed volume of §4.1. A marker records it across restarts.

The log is checked on ext4 and XFS. macOS and Windows have no read path that bypasses the cache yet. Until those ports supply one, a log that was marked failed and cannot be checked against the device is not reopened, and the volume falls back to its last uploaded cut.

#### The home image (M1.2b, M1.3f, M1.3g)

- **Home image.** In M1 the store is the log and one file of the volume's size. A write, a write-zeroes and a discard are each one log record. An index in memory names, for each block, the newest record that writes it, and a read takes the block from that record, or from the home image if there is none. A checkpoint copies durable records into the home image, syncs it, moves the log's start, and only then lets go of those index entries. Three rules were added to that, each by a review or a test.
  - *A checkpoint puts only its own blocks at risk.* A write into the home image rewrites a whole write unit, and a power cut can garble the unit's other blocks, which nothing else may hold. Where the unit is larger than a block, a checkpoint works in batches: it saves those neighbours in a small keyed file and syncs it before it writes the home image. The file is bound to the log's start. The reclaim that ends a batch moves the start durably and so retires the file without another write, and an open writes a file back only if it is whole and belongs to the start the log has.
  - *One instance.* Opening a store is a recovery that writes, so the store locks its directory before it reads anything and unlocks explicitly when it closes (§4.2).
  - *A failure is announced before it is recorded.* The store tells its owner that it has failed before it attempts the marker, which needs the disk that has just failed (§4.2).

  A discard is recorded in the log, for the cut, and changes nothing locally. It is not a way to make data unreadable on the local disk. Four things are not yet as this document wants them: log segments are created on demand and not preallocated, so a full disk is found on the commit path; a file system that fills during a checkpoint fails the volume, where §4.1 holds a condition that can recover; what a checkpoint in batches costs is not measured (§10); and the store still makes a full checkpoint when its log is full, beside the rule below, and whether the rule keeps a served volume's log from ever reaching that has not been checked against the log's capacity.

#### When the daemon checkpoints (M1.3c, M1.3e, M1.3i)

- **When the daemon checkpoints.** A start replays the whole log, so the log's length is the time a take-over costs, and a log that is never checkpointed grows until the disk is full. The first run of the daemon on the kernel showed the first of these: with no checkpoint at all, the twentieth take-over of one volume took 23 s where the first took under 3 s[^ckpt]. The rule, decided on 2026-11-11 and built in M1.3c (skyzfs/skyzfs#17):
  - *Two marks on the log's live size*, the distance from its start to its end. Above the high mark the store's worker checkpoints, in steps, until the log is below the low mark. The marks are counted in whole segments, because disk comes back only in whole segments. The first values are two segments and one. The high mark is meant to keep the replay to about a second. On a GitHub runner the replay read 140 to 160 MiB of log a second, which puts two segments just under that[^ckpt]; it has to be measured again on the hardware the daemon is meant for.
  - *A step is bounded, and requests go on between steps.* The worker executes one command at a time, so a step holds up every request, reads included until M1.3j (below), for as long as it takes: at most 8 MiB of what its records cover written into the home image, or 1024 records, and one sync of it. Every record counts as one, a discard too, so a log of discards is not one long step. While the log is above the high mark the worker runs one step after every request it executes, and whenever it has none. A step takes more out of the log than a request can add, so the log stops growing at the high mark: the client is slowed, and the log's size is bounded, which bounds the take-over. That holds for writes and, at the daemon's record alignment, for discards. **A step that takes less than a request adds does not break the bound** (M1.3e, skyzfs/skyzfs#19; the gap was found by Codex on skyzfs/skyzfs#17). A write-zeroes record covers up to 1 MiB of the volume, which is what a step is bounded by because that is what it writes, and takes one header in the log: a step takes eight of them, 32 KiB of log, while the next write adds up to 1 MiB. So the bound is the rule's and not the step's: while the log is at or above the high mark and the rule's last step took something, the worker takes no request from its queue and takes steps until the log is below the mark. Before every request the log is below the high mark, and it never holds more than the mark and one batch of requests. A hold in front of the log, a failed volume, or a step that finds nothing to take ends the stepping, and the request is executed. The rule's own sync of the log is shown in the status as the beginning of its step, so `checkpoint_step none` with the log at the high mark over several looks, while requests arrive, would be a defect. The daemon sets no limit on the log's segment files, so the checkpoint of a full log (`make_room`) cannot be reached there, and the rule is the log's only bound.

    What a request then waits for depends on what the log holds at the mark. Behind writes, or behind discards at the daemon's record alignment, it is one step, as before. Behind write-zeroes records of 1 MiB a request of 1 MiB waits for 33 steps, which are 264 MiB of zeros written and 33 syncs of the home image, and a read waits as a write does, because the store has one worker and one order. A log filled to the high mark with such records holds 32,768 of them and costs 32 GiB of zeros before it is rid of them. Measured on a kernel, one run per image: the first 128 MiB written to a pool behind such a log took 131 s on Ubuntu 24.04 and 81 s on 26.04, against under 11 s and under 1 s for each of the next four files, and the log stayed within 135,168 bytes of the high mark at every look[^zerorun]. ZFS sends no write-zeroes to its vdevs[^zfsnozero], so a pool does not get there by itself; whoever zeroes the device before the pool is made does. **The cost is removed by deallocating and not by a larger step (M1.3f):** a write-zeroes record deallocates its range in the home image (a hole reads as zeros and holds no space), writes nothing for it, and counts no bytes in a step, as a discard does. A step then takes up to its count of records, and a request behind such a log waits for one step where it waited for 33. The range is deallocated whole and not cut to write units: where it covers its first or last write unit only in part, the file system zeroes that part by rewriting the unit, so the unit's other blocks are saved as preimages first, by the rule for a write; inside the range nothing is saved. A hole is durable when the home image's sync has returned, and the log gives the record up only after that sync, as for a write. Where the file system refuses the call (`EOPNOTSUPP`, `ENOSYS`) the store writes zeros, for that record and from then on: each such record counts its length in a step again, the cost above is back for that volume, and the step in which the refusal came ends with that record. Any other error from the call fails the volume, with the record still in the log. Which way a volume goes is found by the first write-zeroes record a checkpoint applies after an open, with no probe; until then such a record counts its length, so the first step after an open takes them at the old pace. The neighbours reach as far as the write unit and no further, which is enough because no store is open on a file system whose block is larger than its write unit (below). `skyzfsd status` says which way a volume goes and why (`zeroes_applied`). On a kernel, one run per image[^zerohole]: after the device had been zeroed eight times over its 4 GiB the home image held no space, the 32,768 records were gone in 34 steps, and the first 128 MiB written to a pool took at most 1 s, as each of the next four files did. With the call refused by a test hook the first file took 81 s and 4,104 steps, the home image held all 4 GiB, and the log stayed within 126,976 bytes of the high mark at every look. Built for Linux (`fallocate`, punching a hole); macOS and Windows write zeros until their calls are built. **The write unit is at least the file system's block and the kernel's page (M1.3g).** The write unit is the store's one assumption about how far a write reaches beside what it writes: the log never writes again into a unit that holds a durable record, and a checkpoint saves the other blocks of each unit it writes in part. All three of a volume's files are written through the page cache, and a file system writes back whole blocks, so the unit must be at least the block as well as the page. `create` takes the larger of the page size and the block size that `statfs` reports for the volume's directory, and refuses a block above the format's 64 KiB, one that is no power of two, and one it cannot read. Every open compares again, under the volume's lock and before anything is recovered or written: the kernel's page size, the directory's block size, and that of each file the store writes, since a name can lead to another file system. A volume that has come to sit on larger blocks or under larger pages (a directory copied to another file system, a disk moved to a machine whose pages are 16 KiB or 64 KiB) is refused: `serve` ends with status 1 and the reason, nothing is written, the volume is not failed, and a device that waits is left for `detach`. Entries of the directory that the store does not write are not asked. On a kernel, one run per image[^lbsprobe]: an XFS with 16 KiB blocks reports 16384, a volume made on ext4 and copied there is refused with its files unchanged, and a volume made there is served and scrubs clean. Not shown on a kernel: a power cut there, and that XFS rewrites a whole block around a smaller write, which is the premise. What this rules out and what it costs: `statfs` reports the transfer size on network and cluster file systems (NFS up to 1 MiB, CephFS 4 MiB), so a volume's directory there is refused, with no override; a volume lives on a local file system. A unit above 4 KiB lengthens the log: the first record after a sync starts at the next unit, so with a 64 KiB unit a synced 4 KiB write takes 64 KiB of log where it takes 8 KiB with a 4 KiB unit, and the high mark comes up to eight times sooner. That is not measured. macOS and Windows have no block-size call built, so no store is created or opened there until they have one (M7). The rule of M1.3f that a range is deallocated only where the block is no larger than the write unit went with this package, because it could no longer be reached; the value 3 of `zeroes_applied` in the status file stays readable and is no longer written. What that costs a write load is measured at gate G1; if it is too much, the checkpoint moves off the worker, which the store's single order does not allow as it is built.
  - *Only what is durable is checkpointed.* A record written since the last flush stays in the log. Under a client that does not flush, the worker syncs the log itself before a step. That makes durable what the client did not ask to be, which the contract allows, and it is not a flush in the client's order: it marks no cut (§5.2).
  - *Nothing extra at a start or at a stop.* An orderly stop does not checkpoint: it has to be quick, and the next start pays at most a log of the high mark and what the last moments added. A start does not checkpoint before the device is taken over: a long log that it found is worked off afterwards, by the same rule, while the client is served. The rule begins when the device is this instance's and the worker runs, which is before the daemon says `ready`: the start does not wait for a step, and the first request can wait for the one under way. A hold that is released or moved has the rule asked again with no request to follow.
  - *A failed step fails the volume*, as any failed write or sync of the store does. A kill in the middle of a step is covered by the order the checkpoint already keeps.
  - *The daemon says what it did.* `skyzfsd status` shows the log's live size (`log_live`), the steps taken (`checkpoint_steps`), the syncs of the log that the rule made itself (`checkpoint_syncs`) and whether a step is under way (`checkpoint_step`), and a start says how long each of its parts took and how much log it replayed. The status file is version 5 (below); version 4, which added the way write-zeroes are applied (`zeroes_applied`), is still read. The marks are `serve --log-high` and `--log-low`, in segments.

#### Where a request's time goes (M1.3h)

- *The daemon says where a request's time goes (M1.3h).* Version 5 of the status file adds 153 numbers, which `skyzfsd status` prints as `time_*` lines. All are sums, counts and longest values since the server started, so two readings make a window. A request's life is covered with no gap, by two threads' clocks: from the pump fetching it to its submission to the store (`time_pump_submit_*`); from there to the worker beginning it (`time_K_wait_*`, for each kind K of read, write, flush, discard and zeroes); the store's call (`time_K_call_*`); and from the completion being ready to its commit to the queue (`time_pump_commit_read_*`, `time_pump_commit_other_*`), of which the hand-over to the pump's thread is counted apart (`time_pump_taken_*`). Each wait is laid over what the worker did meanwhile (`time_K_wait_by_A_ns`), and the worker's whole time is shown by the same fourteen activities A (`time_worker_A_ns`): idle, the call of each kind, the six parts of a checkpoint's batch, the rule's own sync of the log, and anything else. The six parts are reading the log to choose the batch, saving the neighbours, writing the home image, its sync, the log's new start, and the index letting go (`time_step_walk_ns`, `_save_`, `_apply_`, `_home_sync_`, `_log_start_`, `_end_`); a batch that a write makes inside its own call to make room counts as the step's parts and not as the write. The pump's storing of the acknowledged position is timed, with the number of completions that waited behind one (`time_pump_store_*`). The cost is one read of the clock per boundary and, per request, a walk over the worker's activities during its wait: about 0.2 µs a request for the clock reads, measured once in a container and not on a runner, and not measured as throughput. Not counted: the time a request waits in the kernel before the pump's thread comes back to the queue, and for a write with FUA the time between its end in the store and the pump's own flush. The benchmark reads them since M1.T10 (§9).

#### Reads leave the worker (M1.3j)

- *Reads leave the worker (decided 2026-11-12; built as M1.3j, skyzfs/skyzfs#29).* On a kernel a read spends most of its time in the daemon waiting for the worker, and most of that wait behind a checkpoint step in progress, on a worker that is otherwise mostly idle (§9). So a read is no longer executed by the worker. A reader thread of its own takes reads from the pump and answers them; the worker keeps every mutation, the flush, the log, the checkpoint and the order of all of them, so what is on the disk and in what order it gets there is unchanged, and so is every argument about a kill, a power cut and a replay. What changes is who may look at the index and at the files while the worker works:
  - The index is behind a read-write lock. A reader holds it for the lookup only, never across a read of a file; the worker holds it for an insert and for the drop at a step's end. A read's whole range is looked up under one hold and a mutation's whole range is entered under one hold, so a read shows a mutation whole or not at all.
  - A reader pins what it found: from just before its lookup to the end of its last read of a file. The worker waits for the readers under way at two points of a step, and only there: before the step's first write into the home image, and after the index has let go of the batch and before a log segment the step freed may be used again. The first is needed because a reader that looked up before a write was appended can still be reading that block in the home image when a step applies the write there, and a buffered read beside a buffered write of the same page may return a block that is part old and part new, which rule R3 forbids. The second because the log's segment files are reused and not deleted: without it a slow reader would read a record's place after another record was written there. The order inside a step stays as it is: home image written, home image durable, the log's start moved, the index dropped, and only then the freed segments usable. Between the start moving and the index dropping a reader can still find a record of the batch in the index, so a reader does not ask the log where its start is or which segments are live, as the worker's own read does and would be refused: it takes the file and the offset of the record while it holds the index, and the pin keeps what is there. Nor does it read anything else of the log that the worker changes. Each wait is for the readers that were under way when it began, not for a moment with no reader: reads that keep arriving do not hold a step, and a step holds every write and flush. A reader stuck in a read of a file holds the step for as long, as a read on the worker does today.
  - What a read sees is what rule R3 asks and no more. A read received after an operation was acknowledged observes it, because the reader looks up after the read was submitted and the worker inserts before it acknowledges. A read that overlaps an operation in flight sees each block before or after it; the single worker used to give "after" always, which the contract never promised and the model never required. A write that one read has shown is shown by every later read, because the index only moves forward for a block (to a newer record, or to the home image once the home image holds the record) and a read submitted after another completed looks up after it, on whichever thread. One reader is built first; more than one is a parameter for a bounded page cache, where reads wait behind reads, and is turned on when the model's rule has been run against reads that overlap each other in time.
  - A read submitted behind a write that is held (the log full behind a hold) is answered and no longer held with it. Rule R8 orders mutations and does not ask for the old behaviour.
  - A failed volume fails reads as before. The mark in the log is the worker's to write: a reader that cannot read does not answer but hands the read to the worker, which marks the log and then sends the failed completion, so no failed read is seen by a client before the mark that keeps a restart from serving the volume. A reader looks at the failure before it begins a read; one under way when the volume fails may complete either way (rule R11).
  - A read of a record that is not durable yet is checked (added while building, from a failure of the conformance suite). The reader takes such a record from the page cache, and a sync of the log that fails beside it can leave bytes there that were never written as the record. So a record at or beyond the durable position, as the worker last published it after the sync that made it so had returned, is read whole and its payload checked against the CRC32C in its header: a match is served, and a mismatch is handed to the worker like any read the reader cannot do, which fails the volume. No read waits for a sync, and there is no lock around the log's syncs. A record is entered into the index only after its one write has returned, so on a healthy volume a reader never meets a record half written. The cost is the whole payload read where a part was asked for, up to 1 MiB for a 4 KiB read; it is counted, and the benchmark's load hardly reaches it (one such read in ten jobs).
  - The reader learns of a failure only after the log's marker has been attempted: the worker sets its own flag, marks, and then sets the flag the reader looks at. A read that begins while the marker is being written is a read in flight at the failure, which rule R11 lets complete either way, and it is served.
  - The status file has version 6 (1584 bytes): a fifteenth activity, the worker's wait at a barrier, with a count, a sum and a longest value for each of the two barriers; the reader's own account, over which a read's wait is now laid; the checked reads; and for each thread the moment up to which its sums are complete. A look at the times counts a thread's wait for work up to the look and nothing else that is unfinished, so no sum goes down between two looks, and each thread's activities add up exactly to the time between its two moments.
  - What no test shows, and is stated as such: several readers (there is one, and no parameter for more); that a buffered read beside a buffered write can tear a block, which is the first barrier's reason and is assumed of the kernel; a pin taken after the lookup but before the first read of a file, which no hook can tell from one taken before the lookup; and the order of a thread's wake-up and its publishing of the times.
  Not chosen, and why. Reads executed by the pump's own thread would save both hand-overs between threads and stall every request of the device on one read that misses the page cache, which no test we have would show. Reads submitted to the pump's ring would save the hand-overs without the stall, but the target would no longer execute a read: that changes the contract of §4.1, takes the conformance suite off the path the daemon uses, and is several times the work. The checkpoint step on a thread of its own would free writes and flushes from the step as well, and leaves a read behind the worker's other work and behind the log's new start; it puts two threads' writes between each other, which is what the simulated disk's single order and the cut tests rest on, so it is the larger change and comes, if at all, after the criterion has been measured again. Letting reads pass the gate at the high mark, or overtake queued appends, gains nothing under this load: the gate was never engaged in the measured rows, and cannot be while one step takes more out of the log than the request before it added. The reader's lock and pin are what the two ring shapes would need as well, so this step is not lost if the hand-overs turn out to be what is left.

#### The store starts writeback early (M1.3k)

- *The store starts writeback early (decided 2026-11-12, §9; built as M1.3k, skyzfs/skyzfs#31).* The store writes its files through the page cache, and nothing started the writeback of what it wrote before the sync that had to wait for it. Now each file counts the bytes written to it and not yet handed over, and once a unit of them waits (1 MiB by default) the range they lie in is handed to the kernel with `sync_file_range` and the write flag alone. That is done for the log (records, marks, padding, a new file's header and zeros, and what is left of a file when the log moves to the next) and for the home image as a step applies to it; the preimage file gets no hint. It is a hint and promises nothing: every sync stays where it was, and order, durability and what a power cut can leave are unchanged.
  - The call itself is the daemon's and reaches the store as a hook, so the store crate stays free of `unsafe` and of Linux; the one `unsafe` block is in `skyzfs-ublk-sys`. `serve --writeback-kib` sets the unit (1024 by default, at most 1 GiB); 0 means the hook is never called, which is the benchmark's reference on the same machine and binary.
  - An answer of "unsupported" (`ENOSYS`, `EINVAL`, `EOPNOTSUPP`, `ESPIPE`) switches the hint off for that file while it is open. `ENOMEM`, `EINTR` and `EAGAIN` mean "not this time": the call is counted as refused, the volume goes on, and the bytes are offered again with the next unit or are covered by the sync. Any other error is a failed write of that file and fails the volume. The review's blocking finding was that the first form failed a healthy volume on a machine short of memory.
  - The status file has version 7 (1856 bytes, 207 time numbers). For the log and the home image: the hint's calls, bytes, time, refused calls and whether it is off. For each kind of sync (a flush's, the rule's own, the one that makes room, the home image's, the preimage's, the superblock's): count, time, the bytes written to the file since its sync before, and how many of those the hint had handed over. `skyzfsd status` prints `status_version`, and the benchmark's reader decides by it and no longer by the number of lines, which is what a new `status` reading an older daemon's file had made wrong.

#### The home image's sync leaves the worker (M1.3m)

- *The home image's sync leaves the worker (decided 2026-11-12, §9; built as M1.3m, skyzfs/skyzfs#33).* A step of the checkpoint rule writes its batch to the home image as before and then hands the `fdatasync` of the home image to a helper thread. The worker serves appends, flushes and its own syncs of the log meanwhile, and takes the step up when the helper reports: the log's new start, the index's drop, the second barrier. The helper writes nothing. Until the report there is no next step and no write or deallocation in the home image, and the batch's blocks stay in the index, so a read of one is answered from the log.
  - The file trait has a sync that ends later (`sync_begin`, `sync_end`). On real files each file has one helper thread, started at its first use and joined when the file is dropped, with a duplicate of the file's descriptor; a duplicate shares the open file description, so the kernel's once-per-description report of a writeback error cannot be split between the helper and the worker. No other descriptor on the home image ever syncs.
  - What waits for the report first: an explicit checkpoint, room in a full log, a change of the hold, a stop and a detach (the end of the served store), and at the high mark the next request, so the log's bound stays the high mark plus one request. That last wait counts as a turn of the gate.
  - Three outcomes are kept apart. A sync that fails fails the volume, as the worker's own did. A sync that cannot be begun for want of a thread or a descriptor is made by the worker itself, as before this package, and the next step tries the helper again: a shortage is not a fault of the volume (found in review; the first form marked the log). A helper that ends without a report is the target's end, and the log is left unmarked, because its start has not moved and the next open applies the batch again. A volume that failed for another reason while a sync was out does not take the step up.
  - A change of the hold can now take a held step up and so move the log's start. The worker publishes the log's state before it answers one, as it did for a checkpoint; the first form answered first, and a client could see the old start after the call had returned (found by a test that failed once in CI).
  - On the simulated disk the helper is two points of the worker's one schedule: the begin notes the unsynced versions of each sector, and the end, a seeded number of the worker's later looks away, makes exactly those durable. A cut between the two leaves what any unsynced write does. With no delay the existing seeded tests see the disk they saw. Two mutants are caught in every environment: the log's start moved before the report, by flushed data lost at a cut between the two points, and a home write before the report.
  - The status file has version 8 (1960 bytes, 220 time numbers), its new fields appended so that version 7 is read in place: a sixteenth activity of the worker, waiting for the helper's report, with each kind of request's wait behind it; the helper's syncs from hand-over to report; the worker's waits for a report; and the requests completed while a sync was out. `step_home_sync` is now the worker's part of that sync, the hand-over where the helper makes it and the whole sync where the worker does, so steps against the helper's syncs show a fall-back.
  - The home image's hint of M1.3k is off by default from here on (`serve --home-writeback-kib`, 0), since the helper's sync starts at once and the hint's calls were the worker's cost alone; `--writeback-kib` is the log's.
  - What no test shows: a detach through the daemon while a sync is out (the store's test covers the same drop); the fall-back on real files at the store's level (the file's own test covers it). On a kernel: the daemon rig's table is unchanged with the helper in the path on both images, one run each[^m13mrun]: ten kills at a step's points lose nothing, the log stays within its bound, and in the suite's third pass 49 and 48 of 61 volumes now show a turn of the gate, which is the wait for the report at the high mark.

#### Several readers, and a status thread that sleeps (M1.3n)

- *Several readers, and a status thread that sleeps (decided 2026-11-12, §9; built as M1.3n, skyzfs/skyzfs#34).* A served store has several reader threads (`serve --readers`, 1 to 64, 4 by default; 1 is the daemon as it was, and a store that is not served has none). They take reads from one queue, each on handles of its own, and each runs the one reader's path: the pin before the lookup, the lookup's lock for the lookup alone, the checked read, a mismatch handed to the worker. The two barriers needed no change, since they count by generation: a reader enters under the mutex the barrier looks under, and the barrier's look and its wait are one hold of it. A reader that pins after a barrier began is not waited for and need not be. A read sent after a write was acknowledged looks up after it, whichever reader takes it; reads complete in any order.
  - The mutant in which a barrier waits for one of the readers it found is caught at both barriers in every environment, with two readers released one after the other in both orders; the test waits for the barrier to have looked again, which is an event counted under the barrier's lock.
  - No new status version. The readers' times are summed in thread-time and the readers' moment is the sum of each reader's, so the identity stays exact for any number; a share is the readers' on average. The file does not hold the number of readers, so a benchmark row names it in its arguments.
  - The lookup's lock is now read by several threads while the worker takes it for writing at every insert. That rests on a waiting writer being let in before new readers, which the standard library's lock does on Linux.
  - The status thread took 2.8 to 3.4 s of CPU in a window of 120 s because every post of the pump woke it, also while it only waited out its 100 ms interval. A post now wakes it only when it waits for a change or when a write is asked for at once (a step's begin and end); the interval and what `ready` promises are unchanged, and a reading is still at most one interval old. On a runner it takes 0.3 to 0.4 s.
  - What no test shows: more than three readers on the simulated disk; the readers' own cost, which a kernel shows instead (§9: their CPU together rises from 4.5 to 7.8 s for the same reads).

#### The readers' queue wakes one thread per read (M1.3p)

Built as skyzfs/skyzfs#37, merged 2026-11-12. With several readers (M1.3n) the readers' CPU for the same reads had risen from 4.5 to 7.8 s unbounded and from 7.5 to 11.0 s at 1 GiB. The cause was the queue they shared, a channel's receiver behind a mutex: the idle reader that held the mutex was parked in the receive and the others on the mutex, so a read woke the holder, the holder's unlock woke a second reader, and that one went into a receive on an empty channel, which spins and yields before it parks. Every read cost two wakes and two context switches.

- The queue is now the store's own (`ReadQueue`): a queue and a closed flag under one mutex, with a condition variable. A push queues under the lock and wakes one reader. A reader's look and its wait are under one hold of the lock, which is let go only inside the wait, so no wake is lost between them. At the end the queue is closed and every reader is woken; a queued read is given before "closed", so the readers take what is queued and then end.
- A push wakes only when a reader waits. The count of waiting readers changes under the same hold as the look and the wait, so a push cannot see none while a reader is about to wait. With every reader busy the submitting thread saves the call: 3.55 to 1.99 µs per read with 64 in flight (release build, real files, one run each).
- The channel ended the readers when its sender was dropped. The queue does not, so a guard (`Starting`) closes it and joins the readers already started on every way out of a start that does not end in a served store: a later reader or the worker that cannot be started, or a panic. Codex and the review session both found this; the reviewer measured two reader threads left where the base left none.
- The reader's path, the worker, the pump and the shown times are unchanged. The idle account stays exact: a wake that finds no read stays inside the wait and counts as idle.
- Measured without a kernel (release build, real files, 2,000 reads one at a time with 200 µs between them, one run each way): with four readers 31.4 µs of readers' CPU and 1.99 switches per read before, 22.4 µs and 1.00 after; with one reader 16.7 and 17.2 µs, 1.00 switch both. Four readers still cost about 5 µs more per read than one, and where that goes was not measured.
- Mutants: a push that wakes all (`READ_WAKES_ALL`), a look and a wait under two holds (`READ_WAKE_LOST`), and a guard that does nothing (`STARTED_READERS_LEFT`), each caught. The first is caught by a count of wakes inside a window of 100 ms and not by events alone: a right store can in principle fail it and the mutant can in principle pass, which the test's comment says; neither happened in 652 loaded runs by the reviewer.
- What no test shows: the effect on a kernel. The pull request's daemon rig read as before on both images, and no benchmark row has run this daemon yet.

#### What the kernel has shown of the checkpoint rule (M1.T8, M1.T13)

What a kernel has shown of it so far, run by hand on the code as merged and not yet by the rig's own checks, one run per image[^ckptrun]. Over twenty kills under a pool's write load the log that a take-over found was never more than about 120 KiB above the high mark, the replay took at most 2.0 s, and the start did not grow, where it had gone from 2.8 s to 23.4 s. Under 640 MiB of synced writes the daemon took 72 steps, the log was within 132 KiB of the high mark at every look, steps were caught under way with requests in flight, and the pool scrubbed clean before an orderly stop and after the restart, with its content unchanged. The replay of 81 MB took 0.3 to 0.4 s in one run and 1.6 to 2.2 s in another on the same kind of runner, so "about a second" is not yet a property of the high mark.

The kernel rig shows it by its own lines since M1.T8 (skyzfs/skyzfs#30), on both images[^t8run]. Over twenty take-overs the median of the last five is within 1.5 times the first five's plus 500 ms (1531 and 1534 ms on one image, 788 and 1049 ms on the other; the rig reads the ready line every 250 ms). The log, read from the status five times a second under the kill test's load, and the bytes each new server replayed stay within the high mark plus what one request can add (at most 134,459,392 bytes against 136,335,360). Ten kills, each sent only when the daemon's test hook says that a step stands at one of its five points, lose nothing. And the conformance suite passes a third time with each volume's log first filled to within one 4 KiB write of the high mark, so that a case's first write crosses it and the daemon steps before the case's next request: steps fell inside the case on 54 of 61 volumes, 5 make no write and 2 took no step. The marks cannot be set below their defaults, which is why the log is filled instead. A take-over is mostly the daemon's start, about 850 ms of about 1.04 s in an earlier run, and the start is mostly the replay of about 110 MiB of log. Still waiting on the daemon: what a failed volume does while a request is held.

The rig's first run on a daemon with the reader thread, the pins and the hint gave the same table on both images[^m13krun]. It is also the first time the checked read of §5.1 is shown reached on a kernel: in the overlapping-writes check 45,600 and 46,258 reads found a record that was not yet durable and checked its payload, and in the conformance suite 387 and 2,685 did, with no mismatch in either image. A killed server's count is lost, so these are lower bounds.

A device is not waiting at the moment its server's process ends, and nothing may rely on that (found 2026-11-12, when the control rig failed once on one line). In the driver as tagged v7.0 the release of the server's char device changes no state; it queues a work item, and that item moves the device to quiesced, re-queueing itself while references are held, the last of which the ring's teardown can drop after the process is gone. The rigs read the state once right after an orderly stop and wanted it waiting. They now wait as they did after a kill, up to the 5 s the daemon itself gives a device that still reads live before it takes it over (M1.T13, skyzfs/skyzfs#35); measured so far, the device waits 11 to 136 ms after the process's end.

#### The power-cut replay below the daemon (M1.T14)

The replay rig of M1.T2 records the writes of the pool's own device, which fits a server that keeps the volume in one file. `skyzfsd` keeps a log and a home image, so a power cut has to be taken below it. `tests/kernel/replay_daemon.py` does that (the workflow's "through skyzfsd" jobs, one per image):

- **The stack.** Pool, `/dev/ublkbN`, `skyzfsd serve` built with its test hooks, the volume's directory on xfs, dm-log-writes, a loop file. The workload is the reference rig's: files written and fsynced with a mark in the record at each fsync's return, and pool syncs.
- **A prefix.** The lower disk as it was after the first *k* records. The rig mounts its xfs, which replays the journal, starts the daemon as after a reboot (an empty run directory, a new boot id), reads the device through it, and runs the reference rig's checks on that image: the pool imports, `zdb -u` gives the transaction group, and at an fsync mark every file whose fsync had returned is there with its content.
- **The deep checks**, a scrub in a writable import and the every-copy check, run at the first and the last checked prefix of each transaction group. `zdb -e -bcc` runs on the image at the end of the log.
- **Two budgets.** A pull request checks the marks and every flush boundary and no prefix between two flushes. The full budget, by dispatch, checks every record prefix. The daemon's log is written back before the flush, so a group's first prefix can lie between two flushes, and design §9 says every prefix: the summary says in one line how many of the prefixes were checked, and says "criterion shown" only for a full run with all of them, the ends deep-checked and exit 0. The lead ruled so when the review asked what the criterion means.
- **Controls.** `withheld` holds the lower disk's writes between two fsync marks back until after the second mark, and must be caught by the fsynced files. The reference rig's other controls pick pool blocks in a record of the pool's own device and cannot be built on a record of the volume's file system. A control with the daemon's hook `--recover-short` was planned and withdrawn: the hook moves only the end that is compared with the acknowledged position, and after a reboot there is none, so it plants nothing. A hook that ends the recovery itself early would be a control; the daemon has none yet.
- **A daemon that stops answering.** Each read of the device has a limit. At the limit the rig kills the daemon and deletes the device before it waits for the reader, because a reader with direct I/O cannot be killed while its read is queued and a device with user recovery queues reads while it has no server. Since M1.T14a a control shows it on a real device: `stuck` stops the daemon with SIGSTOP once it is ready, and the read must end at its limit of 20 s; it was caught on both images. The record phase's pool commands have limits as well, and a fill that does not fit the pool is refused before the run.
- **Exit codes.** 0 passed; 1 a verdict against the daemon; 2 the rig's own error.

### The throughput criterion (design §9)

The criterion: tpcb within 10% of the benchmark model on the same machine. What follows is the account in the order it was measured. Each part names the package it led to. The rule the criterion is judged by today is stated after M1.3m, where it was fixed.

#### As first built (M1.T9), and where a request's time goes (M1.T10, M1.3h)

**M1's throughput criterion is not met as built** (M1.T9, skyzfs/skyzfs#24). Measured on GitHub's hosted runners, each row once per run, ratios formed only within one runner[^t9]: the daemon reaches 0.55 to 0.98 of the model's tpcb throughput, the model being the benchmark's row `zfs-slog` with no origin delay. With the daemon as built 7 of 12 daemon rows in 8 runs are below 0.90; with its page cache bounded to 1 GiB, 5 of 8 rows in 4 runs. A single row near 0.90 says little, since two daemon rows of one run differ by 0.5 to 31%. The verdict rests on the four runners where the model itself is fastest (4,900 to 6,000 tps): there 9 of 10 daemon rows are between 0.73 and 0.85 and one is at 0.91. On the five slower runners most rows are between 0.92 and 0.98, with three between 0.55 and 0.82. The comparison errs in the daemon's favour: its files go through the host's page cache, 3.5 to 5.8 GiB of it at the end of a row against 0.6 GiB in the model's, and bounding that cache lowers every row. Nothing was measured on real flash.

The cost is in reads. On the faster runners a relation read takes 0.32 to 0.37 ms through the daemon against 0.15 to 0.18 ms in the other rows, and a transaction group's sync 1.4 to 1.6 s against 0.6 to 1.0 s in all but one of them. It is not the ublk hop: the ublk project's reference server is at 0.94 to 1.00 of a loop device in the same runs. So gate G1's question is answered no, and its remedy does not apply: the zero-copy modes remove a copy that these figures do not charge. Where a read's time goes is measured since M1.3h (skyzfs/skyzfs#25) and read by the benchmark (M1.T10)[^t10]. In ten runs the runners came in two kinds, told apart by what a sync of the daemon's files costs, with nothing in between: five where the step's sync of the home image takes about 4 ms (fast sync) and five where it takes 37 to 46 ms (slow sync). On the fast-sync runners all ten daemon rows are at 0.91 to 0.95 of the model. On the slow-sync runners seven of ten are below 0.90 (0.82 to 0.90), one is at 0.93, and the two of one run are above 1, beside a model row that was the slowest of the ten. The slow-sync runners are the ones with the higher model throughput in four of five runs, which is how M1.T9 came to see "the faster runners". The figures that follow are from the first eight runs, three of them slow-sync with the model at 4,628 to 5,291 tps ("the faster runners" below) and four fast-sync ("the slower"); the two later runs lie within the same ranges or widen them a little. On the three faster runners a read spends 603 to 675 µs in the daemon with its page cache unbounded, and 564 to 624 µs of that waiting for the store's worker to begin it. Of the wait, 492 to 494 µs are a checkpoint step in progress or the rule's own sync of the log, 40 to 57 µs a flush, 18 to 20 µs the worker's wake-up, and the rest other requests. The store's call is 15 to 23 µs and the hand-over back to the pump's thread 19 to 21 µs. A step takes about 45 ms there, 38 ms of it the home image's sync, 2.8 to 3.0 times a second. The rule's own sync of the log comes before only some steps, 18 to 23 times in a window, and takes 320 to 375 ms each time, the longest single stall of the worker; a client's flush, a sync of the same file, takes 65 to 87 ms. The worker is busy 30% of the time. On four of the slower runners a read spends 259 to 280 µs in the daemon: 183 to 199 µs waiting for the worker, 31 to 38 µs in the store's call, 34 µs between the completion being ready and the pump's thread taking it, and 9 µs before its submission. Of that wait, 127 to 138 µs are a step or the own sync, 30 µs the worker's wake-up, and the rest other requests: reads 10 to 16, writes 4 to 8, a flush 6 to 7. A step takes about 15 ms there, 2.3 to 2.5 times a second, the rule's own sync 21 to 24 ms, and the worker is busy 15 to 17% of the time. On both kinds some 870 to 1,010 reads arrive per step: few reads wait long, behind one blocking call, on a thread that is otherwise mostly idle. What makes a machine of the one kind or the other is not known; the benchmark's own test of one small write and a sync does not tell them apart, and the model row's transaction-group sync does in these runs and did not in M1.T9's. The gate at the high mark was never engaged in any of the sixteen rows, and the pump's storing of the acknowledged position takes 12 to 14 µs with 1.3 completions behind it, so neither is a cause. With the page cache bounded to 1 GiB the store's call is 137 to 171 µs and a read waits 63 to 111 µs behind other reads. Postgres counts about twice as many relation reads as reach the daemon, since the ARC answers the others; its mean per relation read was 174 to 197 µs on the model's rows of five runs, 369 to 418 µs on the daemon's unbounded rows of the three faster runners and 241 to 261 µs on those of two of the slower, and the daemon's account of its reads is 77 to 80% of postgres's relation-read time on the faster rows and about half on the slower. **Decided from this: reads leave the worker (M1.3j, §5.1).**

#### With reads off the worker (M1.3j)

**With M1.3j built the criterion is still not met, and reads are no longer why** (skyzfs/skyzfs#29; ten jobs in three rounds, each row once per job[^m13jbench]). On slow-sync machines the daemon rows are at 0.888, 0.908, 0.875 and 0.885 of the model in two jobs, and with the bounded row beside the unbounded one at 0.843 and 0.886 and at 0.819 and 0.845 in two more. On fast-sync machines the unbounded rows are at 0.941 to 0.964 and the bounded at 0.901 to 0.926. One further job ran the daemon before and after M1.3j on one slow-sync machine: 0.878 and 0.914, once. A read now spends 77 to 175 µs in the daemon on slow-sync machines, where it spent 603 to 675, and 127 to 139 µs on fast-sync ones, where it spent 259 to 280; none of its wait is behind the worker, and postgres's relation reads are as fast in the daemon's unbounded rows as in the model's, or faster. The two barriers cost the worker 4 to 45 ms of a 123 s window, the longest single wait 7.3 ms. With the page cache bounded to 1 GiB a read costs about 350 µs on either kind of machine and the reader is busy 35 to 50% of its time, so the bound costs reads whatever the disk.

What is left on slow-sync machines is writes. A write waits 2.3 to 2.6 ms there against 0.8 ms on a fast-sync machine, and 91 to 93% of that is behind a checkpoint step or the rule's own sync of the log: about half behind the step's sync of the home image (36 to 40 ms per step) and 23 to 30% behind the own sync (303 to 345 ms each, 13 to 26 in a window). What makes a machine slow-sync is now known in its arithmetic. Over 36 daemon rows the time of a sync is the bytes written to that file since its last sync, at one rate per kind of machine with no row in between: 176 to 220 MiB/s for the home image and 235 to 273 MiB/s for the log on slow-sync machines, 1,854 to 2,237 and 3,036 to 4,323 MiB/s on fast-sync ones. The store writes through the page cache and nothing starts writeback before the sync does, so in each sync the worker waits while that file's whole backlog is written, on a disk that the pool's writes otherwise keep at about a quarter of that rate. **Decided from this (M1.3k): the store starts writeback early.** After each unit of bytes written to the log or to the home image (1 MiB) it asks the kernel to begin writing them (`sync_file_range` with the write flag alone, a hint that promises nothing), so that a sync waits for a tail and not for the backlog. Every sync stays where it is, and nothing about order or durability changes; the daemon can switch the hint off, which is the benchmark's reference on the same machine. If that is not enough, the checkpoint step moves to a thread of its own, the option put off in §5.1 and much the larger change. M1 does not exit before the criterion has been measured with M1.3k built.

#### With early writeback (M1.3k)

**M1.3k is built (skyzfs/skyzfs#31) and measured so far on fast-sync machines only**, in two jobs with each row once[^m13kbench]. There the hint does what it was built for: a step's sync of the home image goes from 4.0 and 3.8 ms to 1.0 and 0.7 ms, the rule's own sync from 22 and 17 ms to 0.4 and 0.3 ms, a flush's sync from 7.8 and 5.1 ms to 0.3 and 0.2 ms, and 95 to 100% of the bytes a sync covered had already been handed over. One call of the hint takes 80 to 146 µs. The part of a step that applies to the home image is about 1 ms longer with the hint (1.26 and 0.97 ms), more than the calls themselves account for, and that is not yet explained. The ratio to the model moves from 0.978 to 0.982 in one job and stays at 0.968 in the other, which on machines where syncs were never the cost says only that the hint does no harm; the same binary with the hint off reproduces the build before it within the difference between two rows. No file answered "unsupported". Whether the hint is enough where it matters is open until two jobs have landed on slow-sync machines: the log's own sync has time to be written ahead, but a step writes the home image just before it syncs it, and at about 200 MiB/s the hint may not get ahead of that sync. If it does not, the worker has to stop waiting for that sync, either with the whole step on a thread of its own or with only the home image's sync handed to a helper.

**On a slow-sync machine the hint is not enough, by one job[^m13kslow].** The log's syncs collapse as on the fast machines: the rule's own sync goes from 264 to 12.6 ms and a flush's from 89.8 to 4.6 ms, with 99 to 100% handed. The home image's does not: 40.8 to 29.3 ms per step with 95% handed, because a step writes its 8 MiB just before it syncs them and the disk needs 30 to 40 ms for 8 MiB whoever starts it. And the hint's own calls are no longer free there, 1,071 µs each for the home image (8.0 ms per step) and 331 µs for the log, so the apply part of a step grows from 6.9 to 16.5 ms and the step is about 2 ms shorter in all. A write's wait goes from 2,328 to 2,049 µs, still two and a half times a fast-sync machine's; the home sync is still its largest cause (939 µs) and the apply the second (547 µs). The write call itself takes 251 µs against 102, of which the hint is 37, and the rest is not explained. The ratios of that job (0.797 before the hint, 0.868 with it off, 0.842 with it on) rank nothing: its two model rows differ by 15%.

#### With the home image's sync on a helper (M1.3m)

**Decided from this (M1.3m): the home image's sync leaves the worker, and nothing else does.** The worker applies a batch as today and hands the `fdatasync` of the home image to one helper thread, which writes nothing. It goes on serving appends, flushes and its own syncs of the log, and takes the step up again when the helper reports: the log's new start, the index's drop, the second barrier. Until then there is no next step and no write or deallocation in the home image; at the high mark the worker waits for the report before the next request, so the log's bound stays the high mark plus one request; making room, an explicit checkpoint, the hold, a stop and a detach wait for the report first. A failed sync fails the volume as today, and what was acknowledged meanwhile rests on the log, whose start has not moved. On the simulated disk the helper is two points of the worker's one schedule, "sync begun" and "sync done", so the disk keeps a single order of writes and the cut tests keep their argument; that is what decided against the whole step on a thread of its own, which has two threads writing and needs a schedule between them that every cut test would have to be argued over again, for the sake of about 10 ms per step of walk, save and apply. The home image's hint goes off by default with it, since the helper's sync starts at once and the hint's calls would be the worker's cost alone; the log's hint stays. Not known until it runs: whether the helper's sync and the log's couple through the file system's journal, and the superblock's sync on slow-sync machines. M1 does not exit before the criterion has been measured with M1.3m built.

**M1.3m is built (skyzfs/skyzfs#33): the worker no longer waits for the home sync, and the throughput does not move**[^m13mbench]. Two rounds, each one job per image and each row once; one job of each round fell on a slow-sync machine. On the first of those a write's wait falls from 2,035 to 1,150 µs and on the second from 2,192 to 1,318 µs. Every step's sync went to the helper (52 and 53 ms each, unhinted), about 30 requests complete while one is out, and the worker waits for a report at the high mark in a quarter of the steps (77 of 300, 38.6 ms each). The pool's transaction-group sync falls from 1,124 to 895 ms beside the model's 825. The ratio to the model is 0.921 before and 0.920 after. The disk does the same bytes either way: beside the helper's sync a flush grows from 6.9 to 31.8 ms and the log's own sync from 6.3 to 14.7 ms. On the fast-sync machines the ratios are 0.963 and 0.959 in one job and 0.923 and 0.946 in the other. The package merged for the shorter waits and because it is sound, not because it met the criterion.

**What holds the throughput now is no longer a wait inside the daemon.** On the slow-sync job with a steady model row, postgres's relation read is faster through the daemon than through the model (166 to 170 µs against 223, the same 1.36 reads per transaction), and the pool opens a transaction group every 5.1 s in every row, which is its timeout and not the device. What differs is the machine's processor: the daemon's threads take about 25 s of CPU in the 120 s window on four virtual CPUs (the pump 10.5 s, the reader 6.4, the worker 5.5, the status thread 2.8, the helper 0.2), postgres gets 186 s where it gets 207 s beside the model, and the time waiting for I/O rises from 11.6 to about 17.5%. That is one job, and the reading is the testing session's and is not verified by an experiment. With the daemon bounded to 1 GiB the cost is reads again: they go to the disk, the store's read call takes 254 µs where it takes 56, a read waits 222 µs for the one reader thread where it waits 64, that thread is busy 0.52 of the window, and postgres's read takes 337 µs.

**Where the criterion stands, on every slow-sync job since M1.3j**[^slowjobs]: eight jobs, each row once. One has a first model row that the job's last model row beats by 27% and is left out. In the other seven the fourteen unbounded daemon rows are at 0.797 to 0.923 of the model, five of them at 0.90 or above, and the three rows bounded to 1 GiB at 0.819, 0.843 and 0.856. The latest of them, the only one with M1.3k and M1.3m built and a steady model, has its three unbounded rows at 0.920 to 0.923. On fast-sync machines the unbounded rows have been at 0.92 to 0.98 since M1.3j and the bounded ones at 0.90 to 0.94. So the criterion is not met on the bounded row of a slow-sync machine, and on the unbounded row it is near its line with too little behind it to say. A single row cannot say: on slow-sync machines the last model row of a job is 0.985 to 1.270 of the first, as much as what is judged.

#### The rule the criterion is judged by

**The rule the criterion is stated by from here on.** A daemon row is judged against the mean of the two model rows that enclose it in the job's sequence (the bracketed ratio), and no longer against the job's first model row. The criterion is met when, for each kind of machine (slow-sync and fast-sync) and for each of the two rows it is judged on (unbounded, and bounded to 1 GiB), the median of the bracketed ratios is at least 0.90 over at least four rows from at least two jobs, on the merged daemon. The range is reported beside the median. The rule was fixed before any row it will judge was measured; rows from before it are history.

#### With several readers (M1.3n), and what a transaction waits for

**Decided from this (M1.3n): several readers, and a cheaper status thread.** A served store gets several reader threads (`serve --readers`, 4 by default, 1 being the daemon as it is), each with the properties of §5.1's one reader, so that reads which go to the disk stop waiting for each other; that is aimed at the bounded row. And the status thread, which takes a ninth of the daemon's CPU to write a file of two kilobytes, is made cheaper without a reading becoming staler than 200 ms, which the kernel rig's checks rest on. Not decided: anything about the pump's CPU, the largest share; what it is made of has not been measured.

**M1.3n is built (skyzfs/skyzfs#34), and the criterion is not met on slow-sync machines by the rule above**[^m13nbench]. **What a transaction waits for beside the daemon, on the steadier machine** (the same round, one run per row). Postgres does not work harder: its CPU per transaction is 0.32 ms beside the model and beside the daemon. It waits: the machine's time waiting for I/O is 16 to 22% in daemon rows against 6.5 to 10.9% in model rows, and a transaction takes 1.72 to 1.80 ms against 1.44 to 1.58 ms. Reads are not it; they are faster through the daemon (123 to 134 µs against 160 to 179). The pool's log device, the daemon's log and the home image are files on the runner's one disk, and on the other machine every row's throughput, the model's too, follows what a sync of the write-ahead log cost at that moment. The pool's write throttle is not it: every delay counter is zero in all twenty rows, and the transaction groups are closed by their timeout. Nor are the other writes of a transaction, which cost the same to within 2 µs. About 0.15 to 0.2 ms per transaction is not placed; the next round measures how long postgres's backends are runnable without running beside the daemon's threads, and adds a row with the ublk project's reference server in the daemon's place, to tell the store's cost from the cost of serving a device from user space.

#### The benchmark judges by the rule (M1.T12)

Merged on 2026-11-12 (skyzfs/skyzfs#36). `topology-bench.sh summarize` gives each daemon row its bracketed ratio: its tps over the mean of the two model rows that enclose it in the sequence. A row gets a verdict only when both model rows ran on the same machine with the row's clients, measured seconds and `TB_MEM_LIMIT`, had no origin delay, and differ by at most 10% of their mean; otherwise it says NO VERDICT and why. The verdict is one row's, and the criterion stays the median over rows and jobs stated above.

Each row also records what the next rounds need to place the rest of a transaction's wait:

- what a commit costs, beside the flushes of the disks under it (the work disk's counts are the whole disk's);
- postgres's and the daemon's CPU and I/O pressure and CPU time, from their cgroups, between a look before the window and one right after pgbench returns;
- the daemon's threads by user and system time, run delay and context switches, with every `skyzfs*` name and `postgres` kept in every metric.

Postgres now runs in a cgroup of its own in every row; until this package it did so only under `TB_MEM_LIMIT`. Where the cpu controller is on, the kernel shares CPU between top-level groups by weight, so rows from before and after this change are not known to be comparable in tps. A bracketed ratio stays fair, because both model rows of a bracket and the daemon row between them are treated alike.

**System calls per transaction** (the trace; times are elapsed, waits included, not CPU):

#### Without the write log: the in-place store (M1.3r, M1.T18)

The owner asked on 2026-11-12 whether the write log is needed at all, since the product claims the durability of its snapshots and a best-effort recovery point when the daemon dies. The answer has two parts.

**What requires the log is the contract's own rule R10**, that after a power cut the volume is a prefix of the daemon's order with every operation whole. Only a journal gives that. ZFS asks less of a disk: what a completed flush covered is there, and anything written since may be old, new or torn. `skyzfsd serve --store in-place` (M1.3r, skyzfs/skyzfs#43, built as an experiment and merged on 2026-11-13 with the log store still the default) is a store that promises exactly that, named there **the volatile-cache disk**:

- a write goes to its offset in the home image through the page cache and is acknowledged; a flush is an `fdatasync` of that file on the helper thread, and covers what was acknowledged before it was received; a discard does nothing;
- requests run on the pump's thread, with no worker, no readers and no hand-off; there is no log, no index, no checkpoint and nothing to recover at a start;
- a failed write or sync fails the volume for good, and the sync is never tried again;
- what dies with the process is nothing that was acknowledged, because the page cache holds it.

What it does not meet, as its description states first: R10; R11's reopening after a power cut; and after a failed write or sync the blocks no flush covered are left as a power cut leaves them, where the log store leaves them whole. The contract suite reports the six power-cut cases and two failure cases as not met by this store, and a separate test of 200 random programs with simulated power cuts, and as many with failing writes and syncs, checks the weaker promise sector by sector. Owed if it were to become the default: the daemon's failure path on a start that meets a failed disk, the failure marker written on the queue's thread, a mode of the daemon rig that drives it, and no way back to the log store (a flush of the log store does not cover what the in-place store had acknowledged; found by Codex and the review).

**Decided on 2026-11-13 (design, decision D5): the in-place store is adopted and the log store is retired.** The design's §4.1, §5.1, §5.2, §5.3 and §5.5 were rewritten for it the same day. **What the log store's log was for, and what replaces it** (now in the design, §5.2; to be built in M2):

- *A cheap flush.* Not borne out: the in-place flush was as fast or faster on these machines, with less than half the bytes written. Not measured on real flash.
- *The cut as a position in the log, and the cut's blocks kept until uploaded (design §5.2, §5.3).* A cut needs the set of blocks changed since the cut before, frozen at the first flush after the uberblock write, and their content kept against later overwrites until uploaded. The log gave the first for nothing. Without it the daemon needs a map of changed blocks that survives the daemon's death, and a copy made before a block of a pending cut is overwritten. Neither is designed.
- *The ordered stream of M9.* Needs an ordered record of the log device only, which is small.

#### A direct write into a hole, at a power cut (M1.T27)

#### The log store removed (M1.3t)

On 2026-11-14 (skyzfs/skyzfs#69). The in-place store is the only store. Until M1.3t-d `--store in-place` was accepted and changed nothing and `--store log` was refused with a message naming M1.3t; since M1.3t-d (skyzfs/skyzfs#79) `serve` has no `--store`, and any form of it is an unknown option. What changed:

- **The volume file.** `volume`, 60 bytes: magic `SKYZVOL1`, the volume's id, size, block size, the write unit (the larger of the system's page and the file system's block at create), a random identity, and a CRC-32C. Written once at create, as `volume.new`, synced, renamed, the directory synced; a create cut short leaves none. A start refuses, by reason and with the volume not failed: no volume file, a damaged one, a version it does not know, or a log store's `log.super` ("create the volume again"). `log.failed` became `volume.failed`.
- **Failure codes** 1, 2, 3 and 5 are no longer given; the note's decoder still reads them. The pump's acknowledged-position plumbing, compiled and idle after M1.3t, was removed in M1.3t-b (skyzfs/skyzfs#74).
- **A power-cut test of the start's own sync**, now the only one: writes acknowledged by an instance that is killed, a new start, a power cut before any flush; the writes survive. With the sync removed it fails.
- **The status file** stays at version 10; the log's fields read zero, and so do the store's time fields, since the in-place store keeps no time account (the benchmark's `skz_time.py` lines for the store are empty). Whether it keeps one is decided when a measurement needs it.
- **The owner's requirement on the tests** (only tests of the log's internals go): 216 tests left `skyzfs-local` and 44 came, 22 of them ports of properties that had used the log store as their target. The pull request's description lists every deleted test, whether it tested the log's internals or a property of the contract, pump or lifecycle, and which in-place test holds that property now. `skyzfs-blk`'s suite, model and planted faults are unchanged (144 tests). The workspace went from 972 tests to 799. The review found three properties held more weakly than before (a waker called once per completion held nowhere; two ports whose planted breach passed every test) and they were restored, each shown by its plant. The log store's five tests of its time account have no in-place counterpart.
- **What the rigs judge:** on a pull request nothing changed, since they already drove the in-place store and every line about a log read NA. A start refused on ext4 with `data=journal` now changes no file of the volume (the rig printed "new or rewritten: none" on all three images).
- **Found on the way:** a start right after a start could find its own read-ahead still in the kernel's cache and refuse; the start's look at the image now reads alone, without read-ahead. Codex found two more: `create --zero-fill` wrote its zeros after the volume file, and a wrapper did not forward the new trait method.

Not done: the home image's file system is not tested against the write unit in the simulated disk (at the base neither), M1.3ae.

#### A call that returned after the kill (M1.T28)

The first daemon rig run on the direct I/O daemon failed check c on `ubuntu-26.04`: in the conformance suite through the block device, a read found one write's blocks where a second, overlapping write should have won (run 43430825700, volume 60 of seed 0x5ca1ab1e). `skyzfs-blkcheck --replay` reproduced it from the tool's trace, which showed only that the rig's bookkeeping agreed with itself.

What it was, from a recurrence with the daemon's request log and the kernel's block trace on one clock (run 43455086383, pass 3): the kernel issued the second write whole; the old daemon applied and answered it; the kernel completed it, about 2 ms before the kill. The tool's `pwrite` returned only after the next daemon had taken over. A direct write to a block device returns after the page cache of its range is invalidated, and a buffered read by udev, probing the device after the suite's discard, held a page of that range and was itself waiting at the killed daemon. The tool counted the write as in flight at the death, and the model took a call in flight at a death as issued again, after whatever was acknowledged meanwhile. The daemon and the kernel did what the contract asks; what the disk held is one of the orders two concurrent writes may have. ZFS does not take this path: the wait is in the block device's `O_DIRECT` path for a userspace caller.

Counts, check c on the daemon rig since M1.3s, each job a first pass and a pass with the delay:

| | `ubuntu-24.04` | `ubuntu-26.04` | `ubuntu-26.04-arm` |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buffered daemon | 10 jobs, 0 failed | 10 jobs and 10 traced passes, 0 failed | 9 jobs, 0 failed |
| Direct I/O daemon, before the fix | 3 jobs, 0 failed | 3 jobs, 2 failed (43430825700, 43453345076); 40 traced first passes, 1 failed | 3 jobs, 0 failed |

Every failure was the same volume of the same seed. Why only `ubuntu-26.04` with direct I/O is not shown: its udev watches `ublkb*` (640 change events in a probe's discard mode against 1 in the others), and `ubuntu-24.04` issued no udev reads at all during check c.

What was ruled out on the way: the kernel splitting the call, or a fallback to buffered writes. 0 of 13,500 overlapping direct writes were split or fell back (runs 43443374413 and 43447103915), alone, after a discard, and beside a buffered reader.

The fix has two halves.

- **The rig** (skyzfs/skyzfs#59): check c runs with udev's watch on `ublkb*` off, and keeps the request log and the block trace for a pass where a case failed. With the watch off and the model unchanged, 10 of 10 jobs on `ubuntu-26.04` with the direct I/O daemon passed (20 passes; runs 43463180252 to 43463225632). That removes this trigger only.
- **The model** (skyzfs/skyzfs#60): a request in flight at a daemon loss that the lost daemon applied is either issued again or was completed and is not. Only a harness that says a completion can be held past the loss gets this reading, which is the device harness and `--replay`; the in-memory and the stores' suites keep the strict one, pinned by a planted fault. A request is still never lost or reordered against an acknowledged one. What the model no longer catches through a device: a server that acknowledges a reissued write without applying it, when the old daemon had applied it. From a block device's client the two cannot be told apart; the request log can.

What shows the model's half: five volumes whose failures a kernel made (three from the runs above, two provoked) fail under the old model and are clean under the new. The repeat rig can provoke the case (skyzfs/skyzfs#62: the watch on, each device opened for writing and closed every 20 ms, and a reader of each device's last 256 KiB with the cache dropped after each read). Provoked, the old model failed 2 of 20 passes (43476467345, 43479660648) and the new 0 of 20 (43479646883, 43479653721), one runner a run; at that rate 0 of 20 happens by chance about one time in eight, so the runs alone do not show it. Two earlier ways of provoking, without the reader at the device's end, gave 0 of 10 each.

On the first rig run of the merged direct I/O daemon with both halves (run 43492027748), all three images passed, the request log was kept for 61 volumes a pass, and 6 to 11 calls an image had been completed by the old server and were issued again by the tool.

A lesson for the rig is in `CLAUDE.md`: a replay that agrees with the rig is not a verdict on the server.

### Risks in detail (design §10)

The design's risk table carries these four rows in short. In full:

| Risk | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| A call into the local store hangs and neither returns nor fails, as a write or a sync on a sick disk can | The requests behind it are held without bound, the volume is never declared failed, and an orderly stop waits with them. At a failure the daemon's delete of its device waits for the held requests, and with it every add and delete of a ublk device on the node (§4.2) | Since M1.3d a failed volume answers what it holds before it deletes, which removes the wait at a failure in every case but one: the queue's thread stuck in a write of the acknowledged position that began before the failure (§4.2). Open, and not decided with M1.3d: whether a store call that exceeds a bound should itself fail the volume, and what an orderly stop does when its flush does not return. The two places for a bound and their costs: the pump's stop leaves what the store holds to the next instance after a stated time, which costs no data but needs a process that can end with a worker inside a system call; or the store declares the volume failed when a call has not returned in a stated time, which fails a volume for a disk that was only slow. Neither is built; decided when a disk that hangs has been produced on the rig. |
| A pool that only writes does not notice that its device is gone: ZFS retries failed writes without end and suspends only at a failed read (§4.2, measured on the daemon, four runs per image) | The pool stays ONLINE for minutes over a deleted device, spends a core on retries and tens of thousands of events a second, and with its log device goes on acknowledging synchronous writes: 192 MB in one run. Applications are told "written" for data that no transaction group can carry. A node lost in that state loses all of it, beyond the bound D2 states | The daemon says it in every message of a failure or a refusal and in `skyzfsd status` (M1.3d). The agent makes the pool read, which suspends it within a second or two, and reports it lost for this boot (M2); until then that is the operator's step. Open: the surest command, and what export, reopen, clear and zed do in that state (M1.T8). |
| The daemon does not reach the model's throughput on machines whose disk takes its time over a sync: 0.82 to 0.91 of it there with M1.3j built (§9) | M1 does not exit on its throughput criterion. Writes to the device wait about three times as long on such a machine as on one with a fast sync | Reads left the worker (M1.3j) and are no longer the cause. What is left is writes waiting behind the worker's syncs, whose time is the page cache's backlog at the disk's rate (§9). Writeback started early (M1.3k) takes the log's syncs away and not the home image's, which a step writes just before it syncs it (§9, one job). The home image's sync went to a helper thread (M1.3m): the waits fall and the throughput does not move, because what is left is the daemon's CPU on a small machine and, with its cache bounded, reads queueing on one reader (§9). Several readers and a cheaper status thread are built (M1.3n) and the criterion is judged by bracketed ratios under a rule fixed in advance: on slow-sync machines the merged daemon's median is 0.871 unbounded and it is lower bounded, against 0.90. Postgres waits beside the daemon and does not work harder; a third of the wait is a dearer sync of its write-ahead log on a disk it shares with the daemon's files, the rest is not placed (§9). Fast-sync machines, which are what node-local flash is like, have been at 0.92 to 0.98; whether the criterion should be judged there is the user's decision and is open. |
| Licensing | The frontend lives in CDDL code. The daemon is FSL. | Publish the frontend under CDDL and the protocol header under a permissive licence, and link no daemon code into the frontend module (§4.3). A preliminary check on 2026-11-11, the owner's, made with two AI assistants, found the split sound on that condition. It is not a lawyer's opinion. One is still due before the frontend is distributed. The daemon's ublk transport brings one dependency that is not under a permissive licence: `bitmaps`, MPL-2.0, through `libublk` (found in the review of M1.3b). MPL-2.0 binds changes to that crate's own files and is used unmodified. What it asks of a distribution in that case, by the owner's second check of 2026-11-11 with an AI assistant and by the lead's reading of the licence's text (its §3.2 and §3.3), neither of them a lawyer's opinion: whoever receives the daemon as a binary must be told where the crate's source can be had, the daemon's own licence must not limit their rights in that source, and the crate's notices stay. So every installer bundle and container image that carries the daemon gets a NOTICES file that names each such crate, says that it is under the Mozilla Public License 2.0, and points to its source, with the licence's text beside it. That is a release task and not a design change: it is due with the first binary that leaves the project, and a check that the list of crates under non-permissive licences is complete (`cargo deny` or the like) belongs in CI before then. The lawyer's review covers it with the rest. |
