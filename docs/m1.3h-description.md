## Work package

M1.3h: the daemon says where a request's time goes.

The benchmark (M1.T9) has the daemon at 0.78 to 0.95 of the reference, with a relation read taking about twice as long, and the status had no number to say where a request's time is spent. Three explanations were open: a read waits behind a checkpoint step's writes, behind a sync, or behind other requests on the store's one worker. This package adds the numbers that tell them apart. It changes no behaviour: the same order of requests, the same gate, the same steps.

Commits: `06b6dff..1abf896` on `zfs-topology`, five: e23d08c (the first numbers), 944e03b (each wait by its cause; the worker's time by activity), 9308a0d (the pump's side; a test of a split wait), 2e687d4 (from the review: a step's parts are the whole step, two counters removed, a completion stamped when ready), 1abf896 (from the review: a write's own checkpoint counted as the step's parts; ready to taken apart; two faults in the ready stamps). Design: docs/skyzfs-design.md §5.1 (the status file; "when the daemon checkpoints").

### What is measured

All times are nanoseconds of the monotonic clock, since the daemon started serving. Counts and sums only grow; a script takes the difference of two looks at `skyzfsd status`. A `*_max_ns` is the longest since the start and has no difference. K is one of `read`, `write`, `flush`, `discard`, `zeroes`.

**The fourteen activities of the store's worker**, which follow each other with no gap: `idle`; the call of a `read`, `write`, `flush` (the log's sync), `discard`, `zeroes`; a step's six parts `step_walk` (reading the log to choose the batch), `step_save` (saving the neighbours of what it writes, with that file's sync), `step_apply` (writing and deallocating in the home image), `step_home_sync`, `step_log_start` (the log's new start, written and made durable), `step_end` (the index letting go); `own_sync` (the rule's own sync of the log before a step); `other` (the loop's own work and commands that are no request).

| `status` keys | what |
|---|---|
| `time_K_count`, `time_K_wait_ns`, `time_K_wait_max_ns`, `time_K_call_ns`, `time_K_call_max_ns` | Requests of that kind the store completed. Wait: from the submission to the store until the worker began the request. Call: from that begin until the completion was ready |
| `time_K_wait_by_A_ns`, A one of the fourteen | That kind's summed wait by what the worker did meanwhile. A kind's fourteen add up to its `time_K_wait_ns`. "idle" here is the time the worker took to wake for the request |
| `time_K_longest_wait_cause`, `time_K_longest_wait_cause_ns` | Of that kind's longest wait: the activity that took the largest part of it, by name, and how long that part was |
| `time_worker_A_ns` | The worker's whole time by activity. The fourteen add up to the time served |
| `time_step_walk_ns`, `_save_ns`, `_apply_ns`, `_home_sync_ns`, `_log_start_ns`, `_end_ns`, and `time_step_own_sync_ns` (each with `_max_ns`) | The same parts as sums over the batches, with the longest of each |
| `time_gate_count`, `time_gate_ns`, `time_gate_max_ns` | Turns of the worker in which a request was waiting and none was taken because the log was at the high mark, and the time of the step taken in each. **This overlaps the step's parts: it is a share of them, not more time. Do not add them** |
| `time_pump_submit_count`, `_ns`, `_max_ns` | The pump, all kinds: from the queue's wait returning with a request to the store having it (the translation, a first write's copy, the backlog while the store has no room) |
| `time_pump_commit_read_count`, `_ns`, `_max_ns` | Reads: from the store having the completion ready (the end of its call) to the pump's commit to the queue. It holds the time until the pump's thread came to take the completion, and the storing of the position if a mutation completed in the same batch |
| `time_pump_commit_other_count`, `_ns`, `_max_ns` | The same for every other request |
| `time_pump_taken_read_ns`, `_max_ns`; `time_pump_taken_other_ns`, `_max_ns` | The first part of the two above alone: from the completion being ready to the pump taking it, which is the hand-over from the store's thread to the pump's. Counted at the commit, so the counts are `time_pump_commit_read_count` and `time_pump_commit_other_count`. Commit minus taken is what the commit waited for once the pump had the completion: the rest of the batch and the storing of the position |
| `time_pump_store_count`, `_ns`, `_max_ns`, `time_pump_store_completions` | The storing of the acknowledged position, each time it had moved, and the completions that were committed behind those stores |

How to read them, over a window (Δ is the difference of two looks):
- **The worker's busy share**: 1 − Δ`time_worker_idle_ns` / Σ Δ`time_worker_*_ns`.
- **A request's life in the daemon, with no gap** (a read's, as means): Δ`time_pump_submit_ns`/Δ`time_pump_submit_count` (fetched to submitted; all kinds) + Δ`time_read_wait_ns`/Δ`time_read_count` (submitted to begun; by cause in `time_read_wait_by_*`, `idle` there being the worker's wake-up) + Δ`time_read_call_ns`/Δ`time_read_count` (begun to ready) + Δ`time_pump_commit_read_ns`/Δ`time_pump_commit_read_count` (ready to committed). The last splits into Δ`time_pump_taken_read_ns` (ready to taken: the second hand-over between threads) and the rest (taken to committed: the store of the position, if any).
- **The two hand-overs per request**: pump to worker is `time_K_wait_by_idle_ns`; worker to pump is `time_pump_taken_*_ns`.
- **The three explanations, for reads**: behind a step's writes: `time_read_wait_by_step_apply_ns` (and `_step_walk_ns`, which reads the log); behind a sync, and whose: `..._step_home_sync_ns`, `..._step_save_ns`, `..._step_log_start_ns`, `..._own_sync_ns`, and `..._flush_ns` for a client's flush; behind other requests: `..._read_ns`, `..._write_ns`, `..._discard_ns`, `..._zeroes_ns`.
- **Reads behind flushes** is a candidate I had not seen named before this package: a flush's call is the log's `fsync` on the same worker, and ZFS flushes often.
- On the simulated disk the walk that chooses a step's batch is about a third of the step (one run, below). If a kernel shows the same, the step reads its batch twice, and that is cheap to change; it is not changed here.

### How the waits are attributed, and why not by snapshots of totals

The lead sketched running totals per activity that a request snapshots at hand-over and at its begin. The snapshot at hand-over is taken on the pump's thread, so the worker's totals and its activity under way would have to be read consistently from another thread at every submission. I took a way that shares nothing and is exact: a request carries the time of its submission; the worker keeps the spans it has done (end and activity, one entry each, in a queue that is reused), and when it begins a request it lays the wait over the spans since that time. The activity under way at the submission is split at the submission's time by construction. Spans are forgotten when the worker finds nothing more arrived and they ended before the oldest waiting request's submission; with none waiting, the last millisecond is kept, for a request stamped just before and handed over just after.

Not exact in two named cases: a request whose submitter was preempted for more than a millisecond between stamping and handing over while nothing else waited, and a wait longer than 65 536 spans. Both have the unknown part counted under the oldest span kept.

**Under which activity a piece of work counts when it is done inside another** (the reviewer's and Codex's question):
- *A write that makes room inside its own call* (the log full: a sync of the log and a whole checkpoint; not in the daemon, which sets no limit on the log): the checkpoint's parts count as the step's parts, in the worker's time and in the wait of a request behind the write; the write's activity is the rest of its call, the log's sync before the checkpoint included. `time_write_call_ns` is the whole call. (Codex's second finding, 4546473736; fixed in 1abf896. At 2e687d4 the whole call counted under `write`.)
- *An explicit checkpoint* (a command, not a request; the daemon sends none while it serves): its parts are taken right after it and count under the step's activities, in the worker's time and in the waits.
- *A held request's attempts* (the log full behind a hold; not in M1's daemon): each attempt is a span under the request's kind in the worker's time; `time_K_call_ns` has only the attempt that completed, and its wait runs until that attempt.
- The two counters `behind_step` and `reads_behind_step` of the first commit are **removed** (Codex's finding, the lead's preference): they were whole waits, an upper bound for the step's share in one case and too low behind an explicit checkpoint or a write that made room. `time_K_wait_by_step_*_ns` says exactly what they bounded.

### What the numbers do not see

- The time a request spends in the kernel and the ring before the pump's thread comes back to the queue, and the same for a commit on its way out.
- For a write with FUA, `time_pump_taken_other_*` counts the hand-over of its flush's completion, not of the write's own.
- `time_pump_submit_*` is over all kinds, not reads apart.
- The file does not carry how many batches the step parts were measured over (it is `checkpoint_steps`).

### The cost

One read of the monotonic clock per boundary. Store: three per request (submission, begin, end), eight per batch, two per own sync, two per gated turn, two around each wait for a request. Pump: one per turn that fetched, one per submission, one per commit batch, two around a store of the position. No allocation per request: the sums are fixed arrays, the submission time travels in the channel message with the request, the completion's ready time in the message with the completion, the spans in a queue that is reused once it has grown, the pump's stamps in a table as long as the queue is deep. No new lock: the store's numbers go out with the log's state under the mutex the worker already takes after every request, held for a copy (about 150 words more).

Measured here, one run in this container (`clock_numbers`, an ignored test that prints): one clock read 34 ns; two reads and the bookkeeping 75 ns. So about 0.2 µs per request on both threads together, against requests that take tens of microseconds at the least. Not measured on a runner, and not as throughput.

## 5. Reads during a step, and a gate that holds back only appends: the options

No code. What follows is from reading the store, not from measurement; the numbers of this package should choose among them.

**What the order asks (rule R8).** Mutations are applied whole, in one total order; an operation submitted after another was acknowledged follows it; two overlapping operations in flight together may go either way. So a read must see every write acknowledged before the read was submitted, and must see each write wholly or not at all. It need not be ordered against requests still in flight when it was submitted.

**What a read touches.** For each block the index says "in the log, at this record" or nothing; nothing means the home image. A step writes into the home image exactly the blocks of its batch's records, and those are in the index until the step's end (`drop_below` after the log's new start is durable). So while a step runs, **a read of a block the step is writing is answered from the log, not from the home image**, and a read that goes to the home image reads a block the step does not change (the kernel may rewrite it with the same content, which a buffered read does not notice). The step's writes and a read do not meet in the same block as long as the index is switched after the home image is written. That is the property every option below leans on.

**Option 1: a read at the head of the queue passes the gate.** Today, at the high mark, the worker takes no request while steps take something. A read or a flush does not make the log longer, so the bound does not need them held. The rule would be: the gate holds the queue only while its head is a request that appends. The order is untouched (the head is the head). It needs a few lines and a test like #19's with reads in the queue. It does not help a read that sits behind a write in the queue, and it does not help against the step in progress: the worker is still inside it. Gain: at most the gated time (`time_gate_ns`), for reads only.

**Option 2: reads overtake queued requests they do not overlap.** A read commutes with a write of other blocks, and with a write of the same blocks that was in flight when the read was submitted (R8 allows either way; but "whole" must hold, and it does on one worker). So the worker could take the first read in the queue ahead of the appends before it, always or at the gate. With a depth of 64 the overlap check is small. Wrong turns: a flush is not a read (it promises what was acknowledged; overtaking is fine for it only at the gate); and completions already come back out of order, so the pump is not affected. Still nothing against the step in progress.

**Option 3: the step runs on a thread of its own.** The worker keeps appending, syncing the log and answering reads while a second thread saves neighbours, writes the batch into the home image and syncs it. This is what removes "a read waits for up to 8 MiB written and a sync". What it needs from the store:
- *The log shared between two threads.* The worker appends and syncs; the step reads its batch's records and moves the log's start, which writes the superblock and frees segments. Today the log is one object with one owner. Either the start is moved by the worker when the step thread reports "home image durable up to X" (then the superblock's sync is back on the worker: the numbers of this package say what that sync costs, `time_step_log_start_ns`), or the log gets a lock around its superblock and its segment table.
- *The index stays the worker's.* The step thread needs no index: it replays records by position. At its end the worker drops the index below the new start, in its own order, after the home image is written. Until then reads of the batch's blocks come from the log, so **the log's segments below the new start must stay readable until the index has let go**: free them after `drop_below`, as now.
- *The gate becomes back-pressure.* Appends wait when the log is at the high mark and a step is under way; reads and flushes go on. The bound of #19 (high mark plus one request) holds if an append waits for the step instead of the worker taking the step.
- *Failure.* A step that fails fails the volume. Nothing acknowledged depends on a step until the log's start has moved, so the worker can go on until it is told; it must be told before the start moves. The worker's own failure (a failed log sync) must stop the step thread before its next write.
- *The hold, the explicit checkpoint, `make_room`* all take the log's start: they would go through the step thread or wait for it.
- *What could go wrong with a read of a block the step is writing:* nothing, by the property above, **if** two things are kept. (a) The index is switched only after the batch is wholly in the home image; a switch before that returns a half-applied write or, after a hole, old data. (b) A read that found "home image" for a block is not still reading when a later step writes a newer record for that block there: the read was submitted before that write (else the index would have said "log"), so R8 allows old or new, but a read of several blocks could get part of the write, which "whole" forbids. On one worker this cannot happen. With two threads it needs a rule, the simplest being that a step does not start writing while a read that began before the step is still running; reads are short and buffered, so the step waits microseconds.
- *Deallocation* is a write for this purpose: a hole punched beside a buffered read of other blocks is the kernel's business and safe; a read of the same blocks comes from the index (zeros) until the switch.
- *Tests.* The simulated disk is single-threaded by design (one seeded order). Two threads need the model's order made explicit (the step's writes interleaved with requests at chosen points, as the step hooks do today) or the property tests lose their reproducibility. This is the largest part of the work, larger than the code.

**Option 4: reads off the worker altogether** (a reader thread, or the pump's, with the index behind a read-write lock). Reads then wait for nothing but the lock. It needs everything option 3 needs for the log's segments (a reader must pin what it reads against a segment being freed), an index that can be read while the worker inserts, and "a write acknowledged before the read was submitted is seen", which a reader gets by reading the index no earlier than the worker has executed every write submitted before it. It also meets (b) above from the other side. I would not start here: it changes who owns the index, which every test of the order rests on.

**What I would do, in this order, if the numbers say reads wait:** if most of the reads' waiting is behind flushes or other requests' calls and not behind steps, none of the above is the answer, and the flush's sync is the thing to move off the worker's path for reads (which is option 4's ground, or a narrower one: syncing the log on a second thread while the worker reads). If it is the gate, option 1 or 2: small, and the order stays provably what it is. If it is the step in progress, option 3, with the superblock's sync left on the worker at first, and with the test model done before the code.
